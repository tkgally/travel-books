The Japanese, although as a race little given to the outward observances of their religion, are yet a superstitious race, and their organ of veneration is strongly developed. They despise their priesthood, as a body of mendicants who, living in idleness and sloth, fatten upon the industry and thrift of their more energetic brethren; but they yet cling tenaciously to the gods and the traditions of their forefathers. And this feeling will ever prove a more serious stumbling-block to the efforts of Christian missionaries than all the prohibitory laws of the State or the impotent denunciations of the priesthood.

If there is one sentiment universal among all classes of Japanese, it is a deep and earnest reverence for their sacred mountain Fusi-jama—the temple, the grave, and the monument of the father of their faith.[[fnref *]]
