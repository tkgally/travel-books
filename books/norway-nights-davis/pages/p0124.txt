Railways are few, and for those who have time the post-roads are preferable, especially considering the facilities they constantly offer for <i>détours</i> into regions of grandeur and beauty necessarily shunned by the iron road. One hears of many discomforts in what are called the “slow stations”—that is, where tourists are few and farmers have only two or three horses and poor accommodations; but to see as well as to be the beautiful,—“<i>il faut souffrir.</i>”

I am again impelled to laud the friendly, obliging civility of the Norwegians; and as to honesty, Diogenes might throw away his lamp, for his honest man is the universal man, woman, and child.
