Heavily laden mules seldom go at any other pace than a walk, and as they cannot bear the burden of their packs very long, fifteen miles a day is considered on the average good travelling for a mule-train.

Being well mounted on horses we greatly enjoyed our ride to Fort McLeod, even though our daily progress was slow, and though the woods were sometimes so thick that both hands were required for pressing aside the branches that would otherwise strike against the face. The fresh morning air, the peeps through the timber, the profusion of wild flowers, the broad views, when, from some rising ground which the fire had cleared we could see a wide sweep of country, the glimpses of stream or lakelet, partly flashing in the sun and partly shaded by the overhanging trees, an occasional snatch of song, trolled out by some of the company, the procession of riders moving Indian file, now slowly and carefully over bog, or rock, or wind-fall, now breaking into a canter where the trail permits this freedom, now halting to examine some curious rock formation, or peculiar plant, or some trace of a far past glacial period,—these and similar elements were sufficient to render our morning rides pleasant in the extreme. For the sake of our mules we usually camped soon after mid-day.
