In making preparations for such a journey, it was necessary to select men suitable, not only for accompanying the packers, who had the management of the mule-train, but some also that would be suited for the trip down Peace River. Our old friends, Jim and Yessen, who had come all the way from the Skeena, were re-engaged, at their own request, to accompany us to Dunvegan. At first they seemed happy at the prospect of visiting an unknown land, but after a little Jim's heart failed him. He grew terribly home-sick. He had already come to the most distant place that he knew, and when the men spoke to him about the world beyond Fort St. James, he lost faith in the possibility of his return if he should venture further. Suddenly he remembered that his wife and children had no food, that they could not fish, that they would starve if he remained away. What was the white man's gold when weighed in the balance against the tender, clinging affection of squaw and papooses, and the unspeakable charms of home? Yessen might remain if he would, but not Jim. Stolidly he stood the chaffing of all around, and very soon after we left Stewart Lake he would be in the bosom of his family, with strange tales to tell of all the wonders he had seen, and of the offers of gain that he had resisted. This may seem singular in people so little given to express their feelings, but it is quite a common thing for an Indian to treat his employer as Jim treated us.
