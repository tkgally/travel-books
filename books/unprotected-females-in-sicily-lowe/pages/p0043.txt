The family, now in exile, lived on the upper story; and, unless they had been brought up in anti-Sicilian ideas, would be found by any rash morning-caller, sipping their coffee with heads wrapped up in old handkerchiefs or nightcaps, bodies in ragged cotton or dressing-gowns, feet in shoes just good enough to burn over the brass basins of charcoal (<i>braceiere</i>, used to warm and suffocate), until two o’clock—when, gay as gad-flies, they would step into their carriage, take a few turns on the Marina, or walk into the Flora; then, after a frugal meal at sunset, adjourn to the Opera, to see and receive all their acquaintance.

Every now and then a rich family fancies giving a dazzling entertainment, and spends a fortune on it; another may have a fairy festival at their neighbouring villa. On the three days in July devoted to Santa Rosalia, the sea glows with brilliant revelry; but anything like a friendly round of visiting, or cosy meeting, is unknown: education being below par—literature <i>nil</i>—politics the high road to the galleys—conversation <i>must</i> flag: their ardent nature requires some vent—gambling supplies the channel.
