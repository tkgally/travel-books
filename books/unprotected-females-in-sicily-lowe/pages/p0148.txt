[[cont]]at the monastery, we passed into the “cell” (a pleasant sitting-room with balcony) of one of the monks, a gentlemanly person, attired in plain clothes, except the black silk legs and rosetted shoes. He spoke English fluently, and with great animation told us that his having been in our country was the reason for sending him up to say Mass on Sundays at that altitude, the other monks always shifting their turn on him, saying “he ought to be able to bear extreme cold;” their idea of the temperature of the British climate, which, coupled with perpetual darkness, is the same all over Italy. The monks merely make a “villeggiatura” place of their cloisters in summer, when the coolness is delightful. A captain’s wife of one of Her Majesty’s ships stationed at Malta was brought from thence in a dying state, carried up in a hammock, and recovered in a week. She was not the first lady who had had quarters in the convent. Queen Eleanor of Toledo, wife of Roger, one of the early Norman kings, was so overcome by the sight of the piety of the monks who then resided there, that when much depressed by her husband’s death, she decided to live always near them.
