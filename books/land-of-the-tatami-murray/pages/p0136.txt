# Ganjitzu—A Happy New Year.

On New Year’s Eve sails into the harbour of every Japanese home, the treasure ship, <i>Takara-bune</i>. The august passengers on board are the seven gods of luck, of which Fukurokuju with the long head is the primate. In the holds of this vessel are stored the <i>takara-mono</i>, the treasures which the gods bestow upon deserving mortals: the inexhaustible purse, the clove, the precious jewel, the lucky raincoat, the weight, the coin, the hat of invisibility, and the sacred key. Youngsters place the picture of this heavenly messenger under their pillows, and dream of love, happiness, honour, and wealth. It is the Japanese Santa Claus, clad in poetry.

“The beginning of things” takes place on the 13th of December of the old year, when a stew called <i>o-koto-yiru</i> is eaten. The jocund housewives, assisted by their charming daughters and servants, now begin making <i>mochi</i>, a paste made from rice flour; pounded in a mortar and made into dough cut or formed into small cakes, and then dried in the open air until a crust forms on its surface. When toasted and sprinkled with powdered sugar, it is very good eating. <i>Shirozaki</i> and <i>mirin</i>, beverages made of rice, are now brewed; these are sweet wines, that taste well to any palate, except perhaps a chronic gin-drinker’s. The servants arise in the early morning with anticipations of the coming events; for this is the day on which the master distributes his bounty to his household in the shape of clothes, ornaments, and money, which are presented with graceful kindness, and received with charming gratitude.
