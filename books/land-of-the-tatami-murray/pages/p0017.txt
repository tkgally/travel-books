[[cont]]To be hated it needs but to be seen—then loved—then caressed. And, oh! how comfortable it is. The little rascal sees the baby born and the old grandfather die on his bosom. He is all things to all men. In sober truth, from the holiest of Temples, in silent worship, to the wildest orgies of <i>saké</i> and <i>geisha</i>, he is the constant and faithful companion. With reverence he is trodden by bare but always clean feet; shoes he does not tolerate.

These are the dry facts. The floors of Japanese rooms are covered with four and a-half, six, eight, ten, or more of these mats, called <i>tatami.</i> The famous tea-house “Ganshokan,” in Osaka, has a grand room for festivals, containing 140 <i>tatami.</i> Millions of bottles of <i>saké</i>, tons of <i>skiaki</i>, <i>unagi</i>, <i>mashi</i>, rice, etc., have been consumed, and thousands of sober and myriads of drunken men—with shoals of <i>geisha</i>—have been reclining on these mats. It is a gigantic haystack.

In a Japanese inn, where all things are clean and delight the eye and all other senses, bedsteads are not. You will probably ask the “missus” in a nonchalant way:

“I suppose you have no bedsteads.”

“<i>Nedai wa, arimasumai, ne?</i>” [lit.] Bedstead as for, probably is not, ah!

“<i>Nedai wa, irimasen.</i>” Bedstead as for, enters not.

But you have your <i>tatami</i>, and on this the little daughter of the house will place an unlimited number of soft futon quilts, soft as eiderdown, silk-covered, pleasing to the touch; and she will say, kneeling before you: “For repose, your honourable self to recline, condescend.”

The wooden shutters are carefully closed, the paper lamp, <i>andon</i>, is showing a soft light over the embroidered and painted sliding screens; your honourable tea is at your elbow—all is quiet, and you are alone on your <i>tatami.</i>

<i>O—yasumi—nasai!</i> Good night!

The poetry of the <i>tatami</i> lies in its surroundings. It is grafted into the hearts of these children of nature.
