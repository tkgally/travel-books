Here is the statue of the old hero, who defied the greatest tyrant and land-grabber of his time; from Valhalla he must be looking down with a grim smile of content upon the fine valour of his descendants, who are driving the Bear back to Moscow.

In a former chapter I begged to state that Hiroshima was famous for its beautiful women. This assertion I now withdraw. Hakata is the place. Here are seen the loveliest women on earth: <i>houris</i>, with perfect faces and charming coquetry. Dressed in their own <i>Hakataori</i> silks over their inner scarlet silken chemisettes, they are simply irresistible. I had the great pleasure of witnessing a performance at the principal theatre, given for the benefit of soldiers and sailors at the front. The entire geisha and <i>maiko</i> corps volunteered their services. The scenery was superb, and the dancing fine. Doors opened at 1 p.m., and our party was sorry to leave at 11 o'clock when the <i>pièce de résistance</i> the “Tzikush Odori” ended under loud <i>Banzais</i>. We had dinner in our box, and were visited in turn by about a hundred maidens, who partook of our honourable Nihon-<i>sahé</i>, and smoked their tiny cigarettes before their final bow and <i>O yasumi</i>. Children of a day, bewitching, bold, yet shy, butterflies, but—ladies, upon honour, with manners perfect and tact unsurpassed.

We were taken to the Matsushimaya hotel, and felt ourselves at once at home. A finer, cleaner, better Japanese hotel there is not in Nippon. A splendid room with snowy <i>tatami</i> and silken cushions, rare <i>kakemonos</i>, a spray of flowering plants in an ancient vase, a fine specimen of the world-famous Takatori <i>faience</i>, made here; gilded sliding-screens, and many other articles de luxe to charm the eye and the senses. In one part of this large room a Brussels carpet had been spread over the <i>tatami</i>, and on this had been placed a pretty table covered with a silken cloth, and three velvet armchairs. The sacred <i>hibachi</i> with its glowing charcoal was on the table, in readiness for the august guest. The little <i>nesans</i> are on their knees and faces to welcome the stranger, whose first command is: “<i>Furo, dozo</i>”—“Bath, please.” Now off with those light foreign clothes and into a crisp, clean cotton <i>yukata</i>, which the hotel supplies to its patrons.
