[[cont]]or so behind us, was an American man-of-war, tearing at <i>her</i> anchors. All round us, outside the space where was anchorage and absence of surf,—in a word, outside the harbour were shoals of coral reef covered with raging, leaping, flying surf: one of these about three hundred yards behind, and directly to leeward of us, was <i>la lavendera</i> (the washerwoman), upon which no ship could hold together half an hour. Connecting the brig, ourselves, and this abominable shoal rapidly in my mind, I thought how very <i>de trop</i> the <i>Sarah Fraser</i> was, <i>where</i> she was; that if her cable parted, as it might at any moment, she would instantly be down upon us with a blow and a weight so immense that we should part <i>our</i> cable too, and off we should both go to <i>la lavendera</i>.

The “norther” certainly was, and as I write is, tremendous; but, comparing it with the gales of the North Atlantic, I should say <i>they</i> were much more mighty and frightful.

What impressed me most during my first few minutes on deck was—first, our very uncomfortable surroundings, and next the unearthly, fiendish <i>hiss</i> of the “norther.” I never heard anything in gale or hurricane like it, and until the ear became a little accustomed to it it was really terrifying.
