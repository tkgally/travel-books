ROUND THE WORLD IN 1870. A volume of Travels, with Maps. By A. D. C<sc>arlisle</sc>, B.A., Trin. Coll. Camb. Demy 8vo. Price 6s.

This narrative of a tour round the world is intended to be an easy, truthful, and interesting account of the men and manners, of the various objects of interest, natural and artificial, seen in the different countries visited. The entire tour occupied thirteen months.

“We can only commend, which we do very heartily, an eminently sensible and readable book.”—<i>British Quarterly Review.</i>

“Mr Carlisle’s account of his little outing is exhilarating and charming; it makes one feel as if one was at a private view of rapidly rolled off panorama with the assistance of a showman.”—<i>Spectator.</i>

“Rarely have we read a more graphic description of the countries named. . . . The book beautifully printed and supplied with capital maps, is admirably adapted for reading aloud in the evening.”—<i>John Bull.</i>

THE NILE WITHOUT A DRAGOMAN. By F<sc>rederic</sc> E<sc>den</sc>. Second Edition. Crown 8vo. 7s. 6d.

“Should any of our readers care to imitate Mr Eden’s example, and wish to see things with their own eyes, and shift for themselves next winter in Upper Egypt, they will find this book a very agreeable guide.”—<i>Times</i>

“It is a book to read during an autumn holiday.”—<i>Spectator.</i>

“His book becomes one of real adventure and interesting.”—<i>Athenæum.</i>

“His racy descriptions of his very pleasant life and innumerable touches and impressions of Nile Life.”—<i>British Quarterly Review.</i>

“The subject is treated from an original point of view, and we can safely recommend this little volume.”—<i>Examiner.</i>

“As you read it, you form a very fair estimate of the excitements that break a cruise so apt to degenerate into monotony. You realise the amount of peril in the navigation of the cataracts; you hold your breath in the act of being shot down the rapids, and breathe again when your half water-logged vessel has cleared the rush and the whirlpools, and floated into deep smooth water. You have a very clear notion of all the amphibious life of the Nile, Arabs, Nubians, Crocodiles, Buffaloes, &c. You figure to yourself the flotillas of eccentric craft that enliven the water way, from the gaudy dahabeah to the rustic grain barge. You see the mud banks crumbling visibly into the brown flood and the river changing its sweep into the fierce rush of the currents. In short we can recommend the work as giving within moderate compass a suggestive description of the charms, curiosities, dangers, and discomforts of the voyage.”—<i>Saturday Review.</i>
