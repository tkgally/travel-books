Both sticks are held in the right hand, and so arranged between the fingers that they simply become a pair of tongs, and as in cooking everything is “<i>done to rags</i>,” there is no cutting to be done, which with us, makes a knife and fork indispensable.

The dinner was composed of <i>everything</i> except Beef and Mutton, these being two articles of food never, I believe, seen in a Chinese house. The staple materials for their dishes are pork, fowl, and fish, all kinds of vegetables, sea-weed, fungi, and all sorts of curious things. Everything is boiled and comes to table either in the form of soup, stew, or haricot.

It is astonishing what absurd notions even well-educated people in England have about the uncleanly feeding of the Chinese. As there are <i>horse butchers</i> in Paris, so, in some of the large cities of China, there may be found a few shops where dog, cat, or rat may be purchased, but I fancy the consumers in both cases belong to much the same class of the community.
