With so small a staff of European assistants the work of preparation has been very trying—beginning at 4.30 in the morning, and with two hours' cessation in the middle of the day, ending only at 7.30 <sc>p.m.</sc> My hope, therefore, that the sea breezes may be beneficial to H.'s health, after so much exposure to the heat of the sun, reconciles me, in some measure, to our present fate.

<i>July</i> 31<i>st.</i>—H. has sailed. I went with him yesterday, and returned this morning in the steamer which towed the “Kien-Wei” to an island outside the river, where she will remain three or four days, and then sail for the North. She has a crew of 130, of whom only the gunner and boatswain are English, 30 are cadets, and the remainder are men collected from the Sanpans and Junks in the river. Not a dozen of them have ever been in an English-rigged ship before. The appearance of such a motley-looking crew, makes one tremble to think what the result would be, were bad weather to come on. My trust must indeed be placed in “The eternal Lord God who ruleth the raging of the sea.”
