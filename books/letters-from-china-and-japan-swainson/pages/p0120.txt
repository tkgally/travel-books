It may amuse you to see the somewhat flowery style in which one of the Boys asks for prolonged leave of absence.

<blockquote>
“M<sc>y dear</sc> M<sc>aster</sc>,—As it is the filial duty of a son to inquire his father’s welfare after a long absence from home, I beg humbly that you will kindly confer me with a leave to do so, and if so complied with further beg to allow me to come back one week later than the expiration of the yearly holiday’s term,
[[right]] Yours obediently
[[right]] T<sc>ang</sc> S<sc>ee</sc> C<sc>heung</sc>.
</blockquote>

<i>Mr Tang See Cheung</i> and his companions all adopt the English Esq. in addressing their letters, which looks very absurd after their Chinese names. H. is much disgusted when people talk to his pupils in “Pidgin English,” which as you see from the above specimen of orthography is quite unnecessary. Much as one despises that infantine language it saves one a great deal of trouble, for, thanks to it, we are not obliged to learn Chinese, which in this province is a very harsh and untempting tongue.
