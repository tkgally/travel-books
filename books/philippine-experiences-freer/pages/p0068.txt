This may be better understood if it be explained that, except in the more elevated districts, the night temperature seldom falls below seventy or sixty-nine degrees. But even so, one sleeps comfortably and never tosses through a sleepless night as he sometimes does in the eastern part of the United States—that is if he uses a cane-bottomed bed and <i>petate.</i> It is unfortunate that the people at home do not know the comfort of such a bed. He who introduces it to them will be reckoned a benefactor.

There is no article more primitive than the stove of the Philippines—a strong box-like affair about three feet by six, and six inches deep, filled with earth and raised to the height of a table on four stout legs. Large cobblestones in threes are placed hereon, as many threes as there are <i>ollas</i> or pots to be cooked at one time, and so arranged as to support these above the several fires, in camping-out fashion. The smoke rises in the room, blackening the inside of the roof overhead and escaping through the various cracks and crannies in the roof. This apparatus is used in all Philippine households, both rich and poor.
