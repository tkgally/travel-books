He cannot recall how many years he has sat there; it may be twenty, it may be more. Life is all more or less vacant to him; he sits in a trance, as it were, watching a slender stream of copper coins one by one detaching themselves from well-disposed hands and dribbling into his sombrero, with now and then a gleam of a small piece of silver.

In the morning he sees the sweeper with his broom of twigs brushing the street, and he is there to see the lights appear one by one every night, passing from one vacant dream into another. But it may be—and this softens the heart to the beggar-man—that there crosses his waking dream, now and again, a vision of a day when life did hold some small purpose; before he lopped off his foot, and with it some part of his mind, and the creeping paralysis of the will chained him to the sidewalk with the hopeless irresolute look on his face.
