The milk was first handed round. The <i>sheykh</i> just sipped it as a guarantee that it was not poisoned and then handed it on to me. I took a sip, as in duty bound, and passed it on; the soup followed in the same manner, but as no one took more than a sip the lid of the <i>couscous</i> dish was removed and the bowl emptied over the pile.

Each person’s share of the dish was divided off from that of his neighbour’s by a hard-boiled egg embedded in the pyramid of <i>couscous.</i> I didn’t like the look of those eggs at all. They belonged to that class which has been described as being suitable ‘for electioneering purposes.’ I left them severely alone.

That <i>couscous</i> was as full of surprises as those ‘bran pies’ which are given to children to dive into at Christmas time for presents. I never knew what was going to turn up next. Now it was a half raw potato, now it was a carrot, sometimes it was a goat chop; these last we ate with our fingers, and then flung the bones under the table or anywhere else which came handy.

One particularly tough bit of goat which the <i>sheykh</i> dug out of the pile and handed to me as a special favour was quite beyond my powers of mastication.
