We jumped over a small hedge into a buckwheat field, and here it was as hard as iron. The scent was rather “catchy”—either a big handful strewing the ground thickly, or an interval without any; and we were sometimes cantering all over the field, to find, perhaps, that the hares had turned sharp up a hedgerow, and were away on our right or left, as it might be.

S. got into a bog after we had gone across a rice field, and the pony came over on his side, but they were none the worse. We ran on well till we came to a small canal with an awkward place to ride under or over, and a very narrow bridge beyond. Some of the hounds led their ponies under, others over, others were low enough to squeeze underneath without getting off. We tailed over the bridge with much pushing and jostling, and getting into the field, spread out fanwise over it, hunting for the scent, which stopped dead after twenty or thirty yards—never a sign,—

<verse>
Into the middle of the field, and further was there none!
</verse>

Suddenly a halloo back!—the cunning hares had doubled under again by the canal, and were, no doubt, by this time speeding away across the vale. We squeezed and clambered back over the canal, and got on the line again right-handed, galloping all we knew.
