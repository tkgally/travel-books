He was accompanied by a number of men, who sat with a bored expression on their faces while the rabbi intoned more psalms and prayers. This over, they all filed out, we following the procession, <i>en route</i> for the bride’s house, where the concluding and most interesting ceremonies were to take place. (N.B.—The women do not go to the synagogue.) We take up our position in the courtyard, against one wall of which a high platform has been constructed, on which is placed a chair. The bridegroom, still wearing on his shoulders that extraordinary shawl, which reminds one of a chilly old lady, is accommodated with another chair on the ground in line with the platform, the rabbis standing in front.

After a long wait, down comes the bride from her chamber, accompanied by her mother (her father is dead). Both ladies are indeed “a sight for to see,” and I now begin to realize why no visit to Tunis is considered complete without a sight of a Tunisian Jewess’s wedding.
