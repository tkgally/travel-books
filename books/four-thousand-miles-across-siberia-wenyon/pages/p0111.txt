Here it is that the hunters of Far Eastern Siberia find a market for their furs, and sometimes more than twenty thousand pounds worth of sable-skins alone have passed through the city in one season. It will be a busy grain-mart some day, for around it are tens of thousands of acres of virgin soil waiting only for the husbandmen.

It was a stiff climb to the top of the great rocky platform on which the city stands, and it did not, on a nearer view, look much more like a city than when I saw it from below—except perhaps in size, for it has such wide streets, and its single-storied wooden houses have been built so far apart, that when Khabarofka has a population of 100,000—forty or fifty times the number of its present one—it need not cover a more extensive area than it does to-day.

The houses look neat and clean, but everything else is in the rough. Some planking has been laid down on the side-walk, but there has been no metalling of the streets, which are simply enclosed portions of the moor.
