# Chapter XII
[[subtitle]] Crossing the Russian Frontier

W<sc>hatever</sc> dangers, either to liberty or life, may be associated with travel in Siberia, it seemed natural enough to think that, having entered Europe, these were passed. But the Russians are suspicious of strangers, especially of strangers from the East, and I was not yet so safe as I supposed.

Within the confines of the Czar’s dominion there was no interference with my movements. Sailing on the Kama and the Volga Rivers, and strolling through the booths of the great fair at Nijni Novgorod, or round the Kreml at Moscow, or about the streets and bridges of St. Petersburg, I was free—as free as a bird is in its cage. The cage was in this case a big one—as big as the Russian Empire; but, like all other cages, it presented to its inmates on every side impassable barriers and bolted doors.
