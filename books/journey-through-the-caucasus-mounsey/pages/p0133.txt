Thus we wandered up and down for hours in the darkness and snow and biting wind, and an occurrence which had happened two or three years previously to an acquaintance of mine presented itself with disagreeable vividness to my mind. Travelling along this very same road on his way to Europe, he was overtaken by one of these hurricanes, lost his way, and remained thirty-six hours in the snow. He had some brandy and bread with him, and with these and constant movement managed to keep himself awake and alive; but two of his attendants, Persians, preferring death to the agonies of such extreme cold, took opium and never awoke again. The wretched horses ate each others’ tails off, but survived.

Though I of course hoped to get out of our ugly difficulties better than my friend, the probability of passing the night in the snow seemed each moment to increase, and weary and frozen I had almost made up my mind to it, when a blast of wind, more violent and icy than its fellows, wafted the welcome sound of a tinkling mule-bell to our ears. It at once woke me up from that despairing numbness, physical and moral, which extreme cold produces on the human frame, and sent up the thermometer of hope very high indeed.
