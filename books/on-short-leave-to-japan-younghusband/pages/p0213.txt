‘Here is one between a representative of the force and an English blue-jacket:—

‘<i>Q.</i> What countryman are you?

‘<i>A.</i> I am a sailor belonging to the <i>Golden Eagle</i>, the English man-of-war.

‘<i>Q.</i> Why do you strike this Jinrikisha man?

‘<i>A.</i> He told me impolitely.

‘<i>Q.</i> What does he told you impolitely?

‘<i>A.</i> He insulted me, saying loudly, “The sailor! the sailor!” when I am passing here.

‘<i>Q.</i> Do you strike this man for that?

‘<i>A.</i> Yes.

‘<i>Q.</i> But do not strike him, for it is forbided.

‘<i>A.</i> I strike him no more.

‘Exit blue-jacket much edified.

‘The author teaches his policeman not only to converse but to moralise. Thus:—

‘Japanese police force consists of nice young men. But I regret their attires are not perfectly neat. When a constable come in conduct with a people he shall be polite and tender in his manner of speaking and movement.

‘If he will terrify or scold the people with enormous voice, he will become himself an object of fear for the people.

‘Civilised people is meek, but barbarous people is vain and haughty.
