The boats have been made on the same model for centuries. The first impression they give is a resemblance to an immensely long gondola; but, seen close, they are much narrower.

The dimensions of an ordinary boat are forty-two feet in length and only three feet three inches in width, with the same measurement in depth. They are, considering their length, surprisingly light, the wood having to be so thin as to be pliable, and no nails or iron are allowed in the construction. The planks are securely bound with stout wood fibre, which is the only thing that could successfully resist the terrific force of the current.

A boat when loaded will take each journey from twenty to twenty-six barrels of tar. They are placed, one after the other, in the bottom of the boat, which, when laden, is only a few inches above the water level.

The crew, as a rule, consist of two men in the bow and two women amidships, who row during the intervening bits of calm water that occur between the cataracts. The steersman occupies alone the stern.

In the midst of the waiting tar-boats at Waala I quickly distinguished the boat we were to make the descent in. In form it was the same as the others, but her sides had been painted a brilliant red, and where the tar-barrels ought to have been seats had been placed for passengers.

The new boat appeared quite luxurious compared to the very cramped and dirty quarters to be had on an ordinary tar-boat, which, until quite recently, had been the only means available for descending the river.
