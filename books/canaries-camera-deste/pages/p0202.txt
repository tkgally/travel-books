The following morning Don José took us to see some lace makers at work. The Vilaflor lace is the only kind made in the islands, and is needle-point—<i>punto de aguja</i> as it is called; it is by no means a wide-spread industry like that of the drawn-thread work, for it is only made at Vilaflor, and even there by only half a dozen girls.

These few workers are kept busy most of the year in executing orders for one of the big shops in the Puerto, but although they have a monopoly of the lace-making, their prices are exactly the same as those of the <i>calado</i> workers—that is, a peseta a day. “<i>Muy malo para los ojos</i> (very bad for the eyes),” said one of the girls, whom we found bending over a wide <i>berthe</i>, commissioned by a visitor at the Hotel Humboldt.

The lace is made entirely of thread, and starts as a narrow needle-made braid, sewn upon a paper pattern and filled in with needle-point in various stitches, butterflies and flowers being introduced into the pattern; it is very strong and handsome, and the price asked for it seems extraordinarily low; for instance—the <i>berthe</i> which we saw in progress had already occupied two girls for a month, and would take another fortnight, they said, to finish—and they asked a hundred pesetas for it, as the time spent on it would represent a hundred days’ work by one girl.
