An English inn, to be sure, would be more consistent in its style—that is to say there would be nothing to choose in point of badness between its cookery and its sleeping arrangements—whereas at Adéje the two were on a very different plane. Not only were the beds very hard—like those in the lodging-house of which a famous humorist pathetically remarked that he really hardly knew which was bed and which was board—not only were there a fair number of fleas and two distinct smells in the room, but the noise which went on above, below, and around us, made sleep impossible during the greater part of the night. Long before it was light, and almost before the screaming downstairs and the tramping overhead had ceased, the whole household was up and about again; fowls clucked and cocks crowed under our windows, and half-a-dozen children of tender ages toddled about the yard from four o’clock to six, dashing kerosene tins and kettles down upon the stones with loud cries, to which were added piercing howls when they landed a kettle upon one of the dogs.

Fortunately we had intended in any case to rise early, so giving up our night’s rest as a bad job we lighted our solitary candle and dressed, got some coffee and bread, and sending word for our mules to come, started soon after daybreak for the <i>Barranco del Infierno.</i>
