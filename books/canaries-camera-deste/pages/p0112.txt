# Chapter VIII

IN the middle of February we again left the Humboldt for a few days and drove over to Icod de los Viños—some twelve miles farther along the coast—one of the pleasantest excursions to be made by road from Orotava.

Skirting the foot of the <i>fortaleza</i> the road passes under high cliffs cleft here and there by a waterfall dashing down a ravine where the sides are draped with maidenhair or hung with sheets of creepers and long fern fronds. Wild bananas and the calladium-like <i>Colocasia antiquorum</i> occupy the stream beds below, and mingle with patches of yam, planted in damp spots by the peasants for the sake of its root. Studding the rock walls are large fleshy-leaved stonecrops, the flat platter-like rosettes of the <i>Sempervivum tabulæformæ</i> and the cabbage-like growth of the <i>S. Canariensis</i> now throwing up their tall handsome flower spikes. Wild cinerarias in every shade of mauve cover the grassy banks, but the <i>Cineraria cruenta</i>—the parent of our greenhouse cinerarias, and known by the purple underside to its leaves—is not yet to be seen, as it only flowers in early summer.

Barefooted children waylay us on the road, and flinging handfuls of wild flowers into our laps, run beside us for a mile or more, shrieking “Péni, péni, péni!”
