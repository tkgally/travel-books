[[cont]]For does not the human mind, in a sneaking, half-ashamed way, revel in quaintnesses, and abandon itself to the fascinations of that which is droll and strange?

Their lacquer and bronze-work, their inlaying in metal, their porcelain and other-work, is a constant source of delight, sometimes verging on distraction; but the really finest of these are now very rarely met with in the shops, so keen and close has been the search for them.

The effect of European tuition, or even their present close contact without the tuition, will in time, we believe, be to destroy the spirit and identity of the old art, for as the art of a nation is the expression of its people, so when the Japanese cease to be the simple race they now are, will their art lose its old features, and become an impure, bastard art, levelled down to the vulgarity of the common world, and the requirements of commerce, the sole ethics of which are the unelevating ones of ‘demand and supply.’
