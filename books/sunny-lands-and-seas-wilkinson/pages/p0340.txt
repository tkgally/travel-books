The waiters enjoy the name of ‘Hash Chuckers;’ and as they do nothing further which merits the name of waiting, the designation is not an inappropriate one.

At 10.30 we arrive at Merced; having passed all the way along rolling plains and slopes of land, entirely covered with waving grain and occasional vineyards. Here we sleep; and the next morning (the 29th), after a hurried breakfast, we are all eagerly waiting for the stage, of the horrors of which we are yet in blissful ignorance. Soon it appears. ‘Surely they cannot mean to cram eight people into that thing!’ ‘Why, it’s only big enough for four!’ ‘An old rabbit-hutch like that, too!’ Such are a few of the expletives with which its appearance is greeted. But it has to be done, and we manage to squeeze into it, all feeling more or less like ‘trussed fowls.’ We soon ascertain, from the groans given out on all sides, that the jolting will be tremendous; but our driver, with a complacent chuckle, remarks that the road here is ‘as hard and smooth as a dollar,’ compared with what we shall find it further on.
