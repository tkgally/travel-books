We tear ourselves away from Bombay, where we have found all so interesting, and at five p.m. we are in the train for Jeypore, in a most comfortable saloon carriage. All the first-class carriages in India are saloon carriages, with lavatories, and a good supply of fresh water. For half a rupee we buy a large bunch of delicious little bananas, and a basket holding nearly three dozen luscious Mandarin oranges. We soon pass away from the town, and get among the broad waving crops, which look thick and heavy, and which have a great and pleasing variety of fine and deep colour. There is the densest of palm-forests outside Bombay; the slender graceful stems and the cloud of feathery foliage, deep and gloomy—a majestic silence against a deep-toned evening sky—a few gorgeous bars of gold in a sky fast taking its flight to the lands of the West, in an unrivalled blending and harmony of richest though quiet colour: a glow and tone such as no eye at home can ever see. When we have watched the varied landscape fade gently away, and have seen the day to its end—and how short these twilights are!—we are joined by a friend who has accepted our invitation to dine in our carriage with us. We are not without our home luxuries here, testified by a freely passing champagne bottle and ice <i>ad libitum.</i>
