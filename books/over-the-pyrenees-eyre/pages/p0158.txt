I offered them a franc for their trouble; they refused it; but both father and son courteously did all they could to mount me comfortably on my mule. I was just mounted when a man rushed up: ‘Was I not Inglese? Did I not expect a letter?’

‘Three,’ I replied.

‘There was one for me at the post-office; I must dismount and fetch it.’

I went. The post-office was a linen-draper’s shop; the master was absent; they did not know if they ought to give me the letter. ‘Let me see it,’ said I.

It was from Mrs. Brotherton. ‘No others?’ I cried, eagerly. ‘That is mine, but I expected two more;’ and I showed my passport, got the letter, and went back and remounted my mule.

The beast <i>was a mule.</i> It tried to throw me over the first bridge we came to. It would walk on the very edge of the precipice, clearly with the same villanous intent. I began to think it would be safer to walk to the Seu.
