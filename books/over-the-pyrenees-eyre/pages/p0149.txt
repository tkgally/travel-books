There was nothing curious or worth seeing in the place. It was not even very old, having, it is said, been built by a priest, with the money he won at cards. M. Moles afterwards showed me the gaol, a small square building, about the size of a very poor English four-roomed cottage. A few holes were drilled through an iron plate in the door.

‘Surely,’ said I, remembering what M. Poéte had said, ‘you do not keep prisoners in there?’

‘We did,’ replied he, ‘till one day we found the man we put there all but dead, <i>il était asphyxié</i>. Now we keep them on the other side, where there are windows.’

‘Have you many prisoners now?’

‘Two; one for theft, and one for attempted assassination of a sister.’

‘And do you punish murder with death?’

M. Moles coloured. ‘Yes,’ answered he somewhat evasively; ‘the last crime of that nature had not been committed—for they searched the records—for a hundred and fifty years before.’
