He seemed anxious to turn the conversation, and I felt sure the Frenchman’s information was correct. About Andorre and its ways, I had afterwards some conversation with my host, the cousin of M. Moles, <i>dit</i> Babot.[[fnref *]] He had lived at Foix, and now kept a petty shop and travelled; possibly, by the excellence of his chocolate, he might be a <i>contrabandista</i>, or smuggler, between France and Spain. At all events, his cakes of chocolate surpassed the best to be had in Paris, and he was very moderate in his charges, and very civil to me.

‘Here,’ said he, coming into my room the day of my departure, ‘we will make out the bill together. I do not want to overcharge you; I only want to live.’

The bill amounted, for four days, to somewhat less than ten francs. I had not eaten much certainly, but I had eaten nothing at Foix, and been made to pay for a <i>dégradation du tapis</i>, of which poor Keeper, who in his way is a virtuous dog, was wholly guiltless, for the <i>tapis</i> was unsoiled.
