Arrived at the pretty Magnolia Hotel, bosomed in trees and flowering creepers, its piazza sweet and gay with flowers, we look down from our window on the palms and banana-trees in the garden; one of the bananas is in bloom, the blossom drooping like a huge rose-bud of purplish-crimson, its mammoth petals two feet long. We have oranges for dinner such as we never tasted before, oranges fresh as the morning breeze, oranges that seem to savour still of the dew and sunshine. They are pictures to look at, and nectar to taste, gushing with juice, and weighing half a pound apiece. But we do not get such oranges every day, even in Florida, where we are as often served with poor and flavourless fruit as elsewhere. In our after-dinner walk, we buy some grape-fruit, which we find excellent. It is a variety of the ‘forbidden fruit,’ a very large, pale-yellow orange, with a peculiar, tart, semi-acid sweetness.

The natural thing to do in St. Augustine appears to be to bask in the sunshine, or in the scant shade of a palmetto-tree, and eat fruit or lotos, and day-dream in the eastern breeze.
