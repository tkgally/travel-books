We had picked up at Nakh’l two of those waifs who always manage to turn up on journeys of this sort. They correspond to the stowaways of sea-travelling, and are as hard to get rid of when once one is started. The two additions to our party were an Egyptian camel-driver, whose beast had died, and a Turkish soldier who had deserted from Mecca, and travelled with the Hajis as far as Nakh’l. A miserable creature this deserter was; half starved, footsore, underclothed in an old alpaca frock-coat and a linen petticoat, speaking no language but his own, and the butt of every one; turned away from the kitchen by the cook, and from the camp-fire by the Bedwin, shouted at by Yosîf, and pushed and kicked by every one, he was a thing to be pitied. The only person to whom he stood up at all was his brother outcast, the camel-driver, and, as that worthy considered himself to be infinitely his superior, they soon became bitter enemies.
