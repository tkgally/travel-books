When sleeping out you wake with the chirruping of the birds, and the first chirrup sounds faintly on your half-conscious senses, and you listen in a drowsy way, only half aware of the twittering around you, till the whole bush is alive with song and the old sun is coming up like a ball of fire; then you gradually rouse up, and life seems to pour into you, and you stretch yourself and look around. There is nothing to cramp your view; everything is big and bright, and “that tired feeling,” so often alluded to in <i>The Daily Mail</i>, has no meaning for you.

It is here, in the crowded cities, that life sometimes weighs on one like a burden, and that one feels suffocated and flat. It’s the crowd and noise—not the hard times or the work—just the want of space and the continual uproar and movement, that tear one to pieces. It does not matter how far you get into the country in England, the noise of the Strand and the hum of the ’buses seem to reach you; but out in the bush there is silence—a grand silence that you can almost hear.
