Taking advantage of this, I thought there could be no harm in ascertaining what really I had done by examining the boxes. In the upper one there was nothing but the carved stick, hollowed in the form of a small trough, in which they are wont to place their joss-sticks, and an old roll of some woven material on which probably prayers or sacred formulæ had once been written, but which was now so discoloured with dirt and age that nothing could be distinguished upon it. Lifting carefully the cloth which covered the lower box, I saw that it contained four or five tiny trap bowls, clean and bright, full of rice and millet and other grain. There was neither image nor painting nor any representation of any divinity. Yet these, as I felt sure, were intended as offerings to divinities good or evil; for Buddhism in Mongolia is largely tinged with polytheism. Geluga quickly returned, and putting together several articles of mine which were lying about, told me that the tent was put up and that he had made a fire. Desirous of being quite sure what was wished of me I would not take his hint. But presently the Mongol who had invited me into the hut returned in company with several others, and told me that the tent was put up, that a fire had been made, and that I could cook my dinner there.
