Their recitation of the prayers (so called), which are written in Thibetan, the sacred language, of which many of them do not understand a word, is utterly formal, and, if there be no witnesses present, often unreal and irreverent to the last degree. They will frequently break off their recitations to chatter and laugh, and sometimes shout out mere gibberish—“vain repetitions” indeed.

Externally, the great hall of the temple is roofed over with a large dome, made of a framework of wood covered with canvas; so that, at a little distance, these temples appear not unlike large tents, such as are sometimes used in country places in England for the performances of a circus.

In this strange city the most singular feature to the eye of the stranger is, perhaps, that presented by the mechanical contrivances for prayer. Over the gate of each of the enclosures, between the cross-pieces, there is fixed a good-sized prayer-wheel, to the drum or cylinder of which two or three small sails are attached, similar to those that are fastened to the children’s toy windmills in England. These are caught by every passing breeze; so that, especially when a strong wind prevails, the rapidly revolving cylinders cause a perpetual whirr throughout the place.
