## V.

### <i>Trade of the English and other Europeans to, and from, China.</i>

A few years ago, the exports to China, on the Company’s account, in English goods and in English bottoms, scarcely exceeded <i>£.</i> 100,000 per annum. The private trade was nearly as much. The balance for teas and other goods was paid in silver. Since the Commutation Act, the exports have been gradually rising, but are yet far from having reached their highest point. There were imported into Canton in 1792, from England, in sixteen Company’s ships, to the amount of near <i>£.</i> 1,000,000, in lead, tin, woollens, together with furs, and other articles of private trade. The order for woollens only the following year, was <i>£.</i> 250,000 higher than the preceding year.

The value of exports from China to England in 1794, was above <i>£.</i> 1,500,000 prime cost, beside freight and charges of merchandize, and will have probably produced above <i>£.</i> 3,000,000.

The legal trade from the British dependencies in India to Canton, in 1792, amounted to very near the sum of <i>£.</i> 700,000 besides opium, which is clandestinely imported there, to the amount of about <i>£.</i> 250,000. The articles legally imported consisted of cotton, tin, pepper, sandal wood, elephants teeth, and bees wax.

The exports from Canton to India, amounted only, in 1792, to <i>£.</i> 330,000, leaving a vast balance in favour of India, which is paid in cash. The articles purchased for India, consist chiefly of raw and wrought silk, sugar, and sugar candy, tutenag, alum, porcelain, camphor, Nankeen cloth, quicksilver, and turmeric.

The total imports from foreign European nations to Canton, in 1792, amounted to <i>£.</i> 200,000, and their exports to upwards of <i>£.</i> 600,000. Many of the imports were of British manufacture.
