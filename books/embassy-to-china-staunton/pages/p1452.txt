Many of those islands, tho close to each other, were divided by channels of great depth. They rested upon a foundation of grey or red granite, some part resembling porphyry, except in hardness. They were, certainly, not formed by the successive alluvion from the earth brought into the sea by the great river at whose mouth they were situated, like the numerous low and muddy islands, at the mouth of the Po, and many others; but should rather be considered as the remains of part of the continent thus scooped and furrowed, as it were, into islands, by the force of violent torrents carrying off, further into the sea, whatever was less resistible than the rocks just mentioned. Some of them wore a very inviting aspect; one in particular, called Poo-too, is described as a perfect paradise; this spot was chosen, no doubt, for its natural beauties, and afterwards embellished by a set of religious men, who, to the number of three thousand, possess the whole of it, living there in a state of celibacy. It contains four hundred temples, to each of which are annexed dwelling houses and gardens, for the accommodation of those monks. This large monastery, as it may be called, is richly endowed, and its fame is spread throughout the empire.
