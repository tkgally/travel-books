But according to the manuscript account of that voyage, which seems to have been drawn up without disguise, “the procurador of Maccow soon repaired aboard the principal ship of the English, and said, that for matter of refreshing, he would provide them; but that there was a main obstacle to their trading, which was the non-consent of the Chineses, who, he pretended, held his (the Portugueze) people in miserable subjection. The English determined, however, to discover the river of Canton; and fitted out a barge and pinnace with above fifty men, which, after two days, came in sight of the mouth of the river, being a very goodly inlet, and utterly prohibited to the Portugals (Portugueze) by the Chineses, who do not willingly admit any strangers to the view of it, being the passage and secure harbour for their best jounckes, both of war and merchandize; so that the Portugal traffic to Canton was only in small vessels, through divers narrow shoaled straits, amongst many broken islands adjoining to the main.
