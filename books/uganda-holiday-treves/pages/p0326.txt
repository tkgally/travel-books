There is very little Romance in it; the Promised Land has been seen, the wonder world has become familiar, the dream has changed to the tale that is told. The merchant adventurer and the prospector are busy adding up figures in note-books. They were doing the same on the journey out, but the figures have somehow dwindled and no longer tally with those fine totals they smiled over on the Ship of Good Hope.

The most gratifying figure among the northward bound is the young official going home on leave. He is as boisterous as a schoolboy or as a dog let out of a kennel. He is as a thirsty man hurrying towards a splashing pool. The programme which he has pondered and elaborated every week for the last three years is varied and exhaustive. For every hour of sixty days he will live. His mother dwells in a village and does not know by what ship he is returning, so he has constructed in a dozen different ways the exact details of his homecoming. Will the station-master recognise him, will the bus from the ‘George’ be at the station, and where will he come upon his mother? ‘What shall he do when he first goes up to London? Why, have his hair cut, of course, and go to the Club. What ridiculous questions people ask.’
