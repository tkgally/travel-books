It lies in the shadow of a rock in a weary land—a group of lonely houses clustering round the mast from which flew the banner of England. The lamentable path that leads from the houses to the landing place is nearly obliterated. It has known the firm steps of buoyant men eager to make the good name of Britain a power even in Karungu. It has known these same steps when, weak and shuffling, the officer has been helped down to the boat to be invalided home. The paint is falling off the corrugated iron walls and the white ant is busy with the woodwork. The gardens are desolate and choked with weeds, but there are still some flowers that struggle to lift their blossoms aloft, as if to keep alive the memory of the vanished hand that planted them. In a while the jungle will burst into the enclosure and will bury both the flowers and the pitiable houses under a pall of green.

It would appear that the sole representative of the Majesty of the British Government now at Karungu is an East Indian clerk who carries the dignity of his position with composure. We rowed ashore and landed on the minute stump of a pier. At the end of this structure was a flagstaff with a flag bearing the inscription ‘H.M. Customs.’ The banner seemed to be almost as incongruous as if it had been found hoisted on a barren rock in the middle of the lake. The most impressive personage on the landing stage was a solitary Kavirondo native who viewed us with considerable solemnity (see fig. p. <pref>172</pref>).
