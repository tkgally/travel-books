It was of gray granite but had no sculpture or inscription on it.

It was 9 <sc>a.m.</sc> before our fresh horses were secured, and after saying good-bye and thanking the caimacan for allowing us to lodge in the “hall of justice,” we started for Adana. Passing the Pyramus, or as it is modernly called the Jaihan, we rode over the plain in the direction of a high range of mountains whose summits were lost in the clouds. This was part of the Bulghar-Dagh which separates Cilicia from Cappadocia.

We had not gone far before we saw a fringe of trees chiefly oak and ash, indicating the site of Adana. We were surprised, for we had been told it would take at least five hours to reach it from Missis—as a matter of fact it took us only four, although the road was so full of holes that we never dared exceed a walking pace. The roughness of the way led to a meeting, not altogether agreeable, with the truculent Circassian.

We had passed numerous large herds of camels, but no caravans and only few wayfarers, until in about the worst part of the road we came upon a cavalcade of Circassians. The ground was ploughed up into large lumps, and it was very difficult for horses to pass except along the narrow foot-path.
