Though only poor merchants or fisher-folk, they welcomed us as though we had been of their own kin, and struggled for the privilege of leading our horses to the house of the richest man in the village—Paule Dimite. This good friend is a Greek, and he at once got out for us his best quilts and rugs, and made preparations for our entertainment. People crowded into the entrance of the room, all eager to answer our questions about Cyprus. Indeed, I am sure that poor Nahli never had harder work in his life than during the next hour, in the endeavour to translate <i>all</i> he heard.

It was touching to find how these people knew everything about us and our belongings. I had a sort of dread we should hear of Cyprus having been given up, and all the officials sent home; so it was with a feeling of relief we turned to the local news of our own little place.

For myself, I had in one corner of my heart a place that was eager to be filled with tidings of one little pet. I had left my faithful little friend[[fnref *]] and companion behind me; and all these rough fishermen came forward to tell me of its welfare. Everyone knows that the Turks look on a dog as an unclean animal; and I think nothing proved more fully how anxious these poor people were to please us, than their volunteering to give me information on the subject they knew I was most anxious about.

[[fn *]] Readers of my former book will remember my tiny mouse-coloured Pomeranian, “Souris.”
