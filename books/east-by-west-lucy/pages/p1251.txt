A gang of fourteen men were driving piles preparatory to building a structure of heavier cast than the average Japanese house. Standing on a scaffold, the fourteen men hoisted the ram a few feet, and letting go their hold, it fell with whatever impetus was to be derived from the height it dropped. In precisely the same way we saw a gang of men driving piles for a bridge some fifty miles inland.

A common object in Japanese towns and villages is the rice-pounder. A man, or sometimes a woman, steps on the end of a long beam, at the other end of which a stout piece of wood is fixed at right angles. The weight of the man raises this beam, and when he steps off it falls into the scoop filled with rice, by which treadmill work an appreciable portion is pounded. The same primitive kind of tools are in use through all the earlier processes of rice growing. The rice harvest was in full swing as we drove along, and Sunday though it was, there was no cessation of labour, whether in field or homestead.
