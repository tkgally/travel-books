Orders were given to bring in the apparatus for making tea (<i>hiki-cha</i>), and soon a large and rare-looking stove made its appearance, with a round black metal pot and ladle. The pot soon bubbled and boiled; and then the master of tea ceremonies entered with the queer-looking tea bowls, a box containing ground tea, teaspoon, a bamboo whisk, and tea cloth. The bowl in which the tea was made was handed to me. I did not like to drink alone, and said so; whereupon my host explained that only one bowl of tea was made at a time, and was passed round, each one taking a sip. He said that three hundred years ago the tea-soup, <i>or love-cup,</i> had been a very popular institution. Ri-kiu Hideyoshi’s, master of ceremonies, was not only an adept at the making of tea, but had also brought the manufacture of tea-bowls to a high degree of perfection. I ventured to say that it seemed to me to be a pity that so much money should be spent on a custom which was a mere relic of an effeminate age. “I believe,” he replied, “that if the custom were revived amongst us, it would tend to counteract the increasing love for wine and spirit drinking.”

Our host kept a man constantly employed to ransack all the old art shops in the place, and buy up any old tea-vessels he could find.
