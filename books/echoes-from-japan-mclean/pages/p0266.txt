The Thalians are leaving soon, and Sir H. and Lady P., and family, are going to their newly-built house in Tokyo. After coming away from the Legation we called at Mr. C.’s, to serenade them also. We knew that they would be glad to hear those sweet songs of Zion. We were urged to come into the dining-room. It was a pretty and novel sight to see a few of the <i>pride of the British Navy</i> occupying some nineteen or twenty seats, and with uplifted faces, singing heartily. The spirit of prayer and praise rested upon them! Before coming away the young ladies handed them tea, out of their best china. These noble men looked as if they were on God’s errand; and so they were. Any amount of attention, when on His business will not elate. All that the world could bestow could not promote them; its contempt could not degrade them, its passing glory is as dross to those who tread the King’s courts. They wished their kind friends, and mine, farewell, for their ship was under sailing orders.

The <i>Thalia</i> and her band of witnesses have left. Our parting meeting seemed a repetition of that referred to in Acts xx. 38. The ship’s company, according to the inflexible rules of the Royal Navy, were soon to be disbanded, so that they had sorrow upon sorrow.
