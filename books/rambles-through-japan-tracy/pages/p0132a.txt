It is an interesting experiment to approach a Japanese dog unnoticed, and then stop and watch his behaviour. He is lying half asleep perhaps, dozing in the sunshine. Presently the dog observes a strange figure standing near, unlike anything he has ever before seen. You can see he is afraid, and yet he is anxious not to betray his fear. He yawns, looks across the road, down the street—anywhere but directly at you, although you can see that no movement of yours escapes his eye. Presently he finds an excuse to start leisurely off, but the moment he finds himself at a safe distance of twenty or thirty feet he betrays his secret by turning and barking violently, arousing all his companions by his howls. The ordinary Japanese dog must be a very near relative to the wolf; I have seen many which I could not have distinguished from wolves had I seen them caged in a zoological collection. They invariably lift the head into the air when barking or howling. The readiness with which dogs generally notice singularity of dress is quite remarkable. In Seville, Spain, I was once annoyed by a street cur, which for some time followed me, barking at a safe distance; there was no other reason, that I could see, than that my clothing was rather more English than Spanish in appearance, although the difference is exceedingly slight.
