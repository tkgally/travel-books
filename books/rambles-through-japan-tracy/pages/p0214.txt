I do not believe in it. What good to pray to the gods? The rain comes, anyway, after a time. Then the peasants think the gods heard their prayers,” he added, laughing. The young man represents very accurately the attitude of many intellectual young Japanese whom I have met. They regard with supreme contempt the religion of their fathers, nor is it that form of Christian belief which prays for rain in time of drought which is supplanting the creeds they have left.

<i>September</i> 3.—With Mr. Kitasato, who called shortly after noon, I visited one of the largest and most important of the Japanese monasteries, Chi-on-in. It was founded in 1211 by a Buddhist saint, known as En-ko Dai-shi, who was then seventy-eight years old. It is said that various portents accompanied his birth; but these seem never to have been wanting to any one who subsequently became noted for learning or piety in this country. One pretty story is told of him which may be true, although it also has a parallel in religious history.
