Sakai, a city of about forty thousand inhabitants, was reached about noon. Approached from the east it has a fine appearance, the numerous temples rising above the general uniform level of other houses. While at dinner a band of pilgrims entered the inn. The first to come in discovered me, and, instead of passing on, he stood opposite and whispered to each wayfarer as he passed by. Then the pilgrim would stare about him till his eyes met mine, and he had satisfied curiosity. Three centuries ago Sakai was the centre of one of the most flourishing Christian missions in all Japan.

Two hours’ ride along a broad and level road brought me to Ozaka. There are one or two hotels where an imitation of foreign accommodation can be had; but I will have none of them while in Japan. Thanks to my friend Yangimoto, I had been furnished with the address of a quiet inn and restaurant in the southern part of the city, and to this we made our way by dint of many questions. As in Kioto, the approach to it is uninviting, being through narrow streets, chiefly occupied by singing girls; but one view from the window of the room decided me immediately in its favour.
