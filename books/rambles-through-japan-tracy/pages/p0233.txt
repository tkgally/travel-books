One picture represents a heavy rock fallen on a man; neighbours try vainly to lift it; some wring their hands; one old woman, however, is seen praying to Kwannon, and that her aid was given this picture at her shrine abundantly testifies. Another scene illustrates the escape of a mad bull; it has kicked down its hut; everybody is rushing away; a mother with a child on her back prays to Kwannon as she flies for safety, and it is evident that a refuge was gained, or the picture would not as a thank-offering be here. The old Japanese faith in the gods and their pitying watchfulness over mankind was implicit, earnest, and undoubting; will the new imported philosophy which sneers at her religion as superstition, and talks about “prayer gauges,” make her people happier or their morals purer? That is the question for the future.

<i>September</i> 7.—Slept fairly, disturbed only by pilgrims’ chants, which lasted until a late hour last night and began early this morning. The hotel bill when presented showed evidence of my karuma-man’s thoughtful interest; it consisted of two charges, one for lodging and another for food—as if I were ignorant of the fact that the <i>hatago</i>, or charge made at inns, always includes both supper and breakfast. At Kioto, where I had an entire floor to myself, a separate charge was made; and this fact probably my guide ascertained, as he was present when I paid the last account.
