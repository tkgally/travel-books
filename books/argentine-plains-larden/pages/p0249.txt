About December 16, 1888, a terrible occurrence took place in a railway-cutting between Candalaria and Guardía de la Esquina. A cutting was filled with this drifted grass. In the morning a train passed safely through it, though the grass caught fire. But in the afternoon, the cutting being again filled, the train returned <i>with the wind.</i> The drifted paja voladora caught fire again, and the flame ran with the train. In five minutes this was on fire; eight persons were so consumed that not even bones were found, and eighteen were nearly burnt to death—I cannot now say whether they survived. My brother saw one young man who had lost his hands and whose face was unrecognisable; and whether the above account be quite accurate or not, there is no doubt that the horror of that cutting, “like an oven,” could hardly be exaggerated.

Of grasses in general on the natural camp the <i>puna</i> was the commonest about Santa Isabel; it was thin and wiry and grew in huge tufts, not unpicturesque. But I well remember great sheets of grass (<i>paja colorada</i>, I think) having a red seed; how wonderful it looked about sunset!
