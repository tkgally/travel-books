The big noose is whirled round the head, its weight, and that of the iron ring (which is some three feet from the hand), giving it “go” enough in spite of the very considerable air-resistance. [See frontispiece.] When it is discharged the few coils held in the left hand are also in the end let go, and the lazo is finally left attached to the ring on the saddle only. The animal is made to pull the noose tight, the horseman merely hanging back or to one side.

In 1888, when there was much lazoing of full-grown “native” animals done at Santa Isabel, I remember that I was very much disappointed with what I saw; one reads such wonderful accounts of what gauchos do! I believe that there <i>are</i> very striking exhibitions given by picked cowboys of Anglo-Saxon race from North America, who perform for pay; but that is another matter from the regular run of Argentine lazoing.

Here the animal was pursued at a very short distance, and the lazo flung when there was but little relative motion.
