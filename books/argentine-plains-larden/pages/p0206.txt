It—the male, I imagine—has a curious habit of soaring up some twenty yards or so, and then descending with wings spread, tilted somewhat upwards and motionless, and tail expanded wide, uttering a pretty phrase or two of song; taking to flying again when it nears the ground. I suppose that it is showing off to the female; and I take these latter to be browner birds that I saw, having a much pinker, or less crimson, breast. I trust that women will not have these beautiful and harmless little birds slaughtered for the adornment of their hats. In spring and summer these birds go in pairs, but in autumn and winter they associate in considerable flocks.

## <i>The Common Stork, or Cigüeña.</i>

These birds are (from tip of beak to tip of tail) 4 feet 1½ inches long; spread of wings, 7 feet 7½ inches; length of leg, 2 feet 2½ inches. The general impression that one gets of them is that they are handsome birds, mainly black and white. Like all the long-necked and long-legged birds that I know, except the heron, they fly with both neck and legs extended. This bird is one of those that, when once high up, continue to rise in spirals without flapping their wings.
