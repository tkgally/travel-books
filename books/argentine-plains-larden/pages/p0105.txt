Labour is scarce and dear; and therefore the colonist and his sons (or partners) plough and sow the land in successive pieces, and so are able to harvest it in successive pieces also. In Argentina the summer is of generous length, and there is little danger of running short of harvest-weather.

There are sometimes other ways in which the colonists can exchange their one great asset—viz., patient labour—for money or its equivalent. I found that where part of the land was too poor for crops of alfalfa—it was the <i>tierra blanca</i> that lay in the hollows—my brother paid the colonists about 17s. 6d. per 4⅕ acres (or $10 per “square”) to plough and harrow the soil and to sow it with rye-grass seed, which he supplied. Thus, instead of letting this land lie idle, he got a valuable winter-pasture that came in most usefully when the alfalfa was withered and brown; so that he was not obliged to sell off his stock on the approach of winter if prices were low. And the colonist could thus turn idle days to profit. Again, it is necessary—having regard to the devastation due to the locusts, as also to the problem of winter feeding—to understock the paddocks.
