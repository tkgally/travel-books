Some Spanish words turned directly into English forms may enrich our language. But I only came across one such desirable (or, at any rate, unobjectionable) case of this influence of Spanish on English. The Spanish word <i>aquerenciar</i> is used in Argentina of so attaching a cow to a certain camp that she no longer seeks to leave it; thus a cow becomes <i>aquerenciada</i> to a new camp when she has had a calf there. So when an English wife has become reconciled to her estancia life and has settled down she may be said to be <i>querenched</i> to it. The word is not pretty, but it is expressive and not barbarous; it represents, quite fairly, the assimilative powers which are so remarkable a feature of our language.

## <i>Denationalisation of Immigrants.</i>

In this matter I can only speak of my experiences in “the camp,” and so I pack this note also into the present miscellaneous chapter.

I remember how often I have met Swiss and Italians from the United States and elsewhere drawn back to their native countries by home-sickness, and how New Zealanders and other dwellers in Dominions or Colonies look to England as “home.”

In Argentina, in the camp, I did not find this <i>Heimweh</i>. Neither Swiss, nor Italians, nor English seem to prefer “home” to Argentina.
