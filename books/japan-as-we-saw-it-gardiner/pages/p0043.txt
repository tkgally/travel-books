The lunch we brought with us is eaten at a clean little inn called Tsuta-ya (<i>Tsoo-tah-yah</i>), situated on the edge of the lake, from which, looking across the water and beyond its surrounding hills, we have a fine view of snow-capped Fuji. The village of Hakone is about a mile farther down the road, but it is hardly worth visiting.

[[image: Torii gateway and stone lanterns among pine trees, with a lake and hills beyond; lettered “Approaching Hakone”]]

Pedestrians vary the return trip to Miyanoshita by taking a boat on the lake to the village of Shin-yu (<i>Shin-yoo</i>), and thence over the hills by Ojigoku, down to the hotel.

Miyanoshita, with its excellent hotels, numberless walks, mountain climbs, beautiful scenery, and delightful atmosphere, may well consume a week’s time, but as we have engaged accommodations at Atami (twenty-nine miles by jinrikisha, two men, two yen) we leave the Fujiya with regret, accompanied by the “sayonaras” (<i>sah-yoh-nah-rahs</i>), “good-byes,” of the pretty little hotel girls assembled on the stone steps at the entrance.

[[image: A lake with a boat, buildings on the shore and on a hill, trees and a mountain behind; lettered “Hakone lake”]]

The return ride down the mountain road is if possible more charming than its ascent, and all too quickly ended.

Thence through Yumoto to Odawara (five miles) upon the highway following the base of the line of hills on our left, with the river and tram-car track over which we had come on our right. The road, in stretches, was quite rough for want of care, showing how quickly the modern modes of transit lead the natives to abandon the jinrikisha, and at the same time to lose interest in their otherwise excellent roads.
