[[cont]]“Some poor fellow wants to come aboard,” said the mate.

To avoid the severity of the conscription laws, the young men of the Azores seek to escape from the islands on American vessels. They kindle a bonfire as a signal for a boat. They are taken on board without passports, and stow themselves away among the cargo, out of sight of the custom-house officers. Last year a thousand were carried off in this way. We asked our captain, later, if he ever took them. “Of course not,” he said innocently; “but I noticed one singular thing on my last trip: I had passports for seventy steerage passengers, and I landed one hundred and sixteen in America. It beat all, how they counted out so!”

Late in the afternoon we made the port of Flores. Three or four lighters put out for the ship. These island boats are made for rough water, and are so big and heavy that they look like the dismantled hulks of small schooners. They are painted black or a dingy red. No two of their four oars ever touch the water together; and, as they crawl clumsily along in the distance, they look like huge water beetles struggling in the billows.
