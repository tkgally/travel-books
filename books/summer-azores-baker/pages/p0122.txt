Some of these improvisations hinted at infelicitous matrimonial experience on the part of the singer. “If my wife dies,” said one, “I will tie a ribbon on her, and get another much younger and handsomer.” Some had a poetical East Indian flavor: “I have seen the sun rise in the morning from the flower of the water-melon; and this is why he is so yellow during the day.” The simplicity of the subjects, and the <i>näiveté</i> of the expression, were a striking contrast to our conventionalism.

One, gayly attired like a Spanish brigand, in corduroy breeches, a black braided jacket bordered with velvet, and a broad scarlet sash, stamped as he danced, and sang in stentorian voice, “When I sing the little seeds all jump out of the ground.” All laughed at his elation, except our hostess, who frowned as if she feared some indecorum on his part.

The “<i>charamba</i>” ended, the “<i>saudade</i>,” a sad strain on the viola, followed.
