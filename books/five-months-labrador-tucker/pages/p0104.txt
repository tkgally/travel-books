An arm of the sea puts up between two cliffs on the southerly coast, on the right of which stands a signal house, occupied by the British during the last war. Near this are the crumbling remains of an old fort, forming nearly a semi-circle, near which a rude monument pointed out the graves of twelve persons there buried.

Noticing the very superficial covering of earth beneath which the mouldering bones of these dead men were deposited, I was led to inquire into the reason for such a seeming neglect of suitable burial. The cause was explained to me, when I was told that the soil, on that part of the coast, was scarcely in any place found to be more than eighteen to twenty-four inches deep. The whole peninsula seems to be composed of a solid mass of stone, with only a thin stratum of earth, gathered upon its surface during the lapse of ages.
