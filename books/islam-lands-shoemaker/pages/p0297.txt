Only sixty years ago, a mausoleum, built by some Numidian who died before our era, stood in almost perfect condition. It held, on two stones, a remarkable bi-lingual inscription in the Libyan and Punic tongues, the only known example in Northern Africa.

In 1842, Sir T. Reade, the then H. B. M. Consul-General at Tunis, obtained permission to remove these stones. It was done, but, instead of looking after the work, and sparing the structure, he entrusted it to ignorant, clumsy Arabs, who demolished the mausoleum. The stones are in the British Museum,—of the mausoleum only a few steps now remain.

There is great work in excavating yet to be done in Dougga. As we walk over the hill, its ruins crop up thickly all around us, but the day passes and we enter the motor and move on, leaving the dead city to the Arab dogs just now howling a noisy requiem all over it.

We hold a flying course over valleys and mountains to Souk-el-Arba, which is two hundred kilometres from Tunis, and where we have luncheon.
