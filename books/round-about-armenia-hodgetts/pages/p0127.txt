One of my informants belonged to a family which had had ten male members; now only five remained living. The others had suffered similar bereavements. One of the peasants had lost sight of his wife during his flight. They were a newly-married couple, and her loss had greatly afflicted him. But in a village in Russian Armenia he found her again. She had managed to get there before him, and one can imagine the joy of this unexpected meeting. Each had given up the other for lost.

I was told that the entire district of Sassun was desolate; that there was not a habitation left standing except the hut over the pit at Galeguzan; and, as the peasants vividly expressed it, that there was not even a sparrow to be found there.

I naturally asked the peasants what their object was in coming to Russia, and what they proposed to do?

They told me that they had come to Russia for help—that is to say, to collect funds to purchase weapons with, and with the intention of returning to Sassun in the spring, in order to avenge themselves on the Turks. They told me that they had nothing left to live for, that they had lost their parents, their relations, their flocks, and that revenge was now the one animating passion of their lives.
