The ancients evidently had no such scruples, for Jael was blessed and her deed set forth in Deborah’s song.

Passing on we skirted a slope of the Hill Mizar, and saw the village of Nain, where the widow’s son was brought back to life. The place was doubtless much the same in our Lord’s time as it is to-day, neither better nor worse than a hundred other hill villages along the route.

A short distance beyond Nain is Shunem, where I was shown a house said to be the same in which “a little chamber in the wall” was built for the Prophet Elisha. The house is now a square stone ruin, and is used as a guest-house for pilgrims. We saw a flashily dressed man breakfasting in the doorway, while a score of villagers waited at a respectful distance until he should finish. This man was tax-collector for the district, which accounted for the respect shown him.

The fields about Shunem are still used for farming as much as at the time when the Shunammite woman’s son went out to watch the reapers and was sunstruck. Sunstroke is not uncommon in this shadeless plain.

We traversed fields where men were guiding primitive plows drawn sometimes by a cow, sometimes by a camel, but oftenest by a bullock and a donkey yoked together. Other fellaheen came behind, sowing; holding the grain in bags formed by gathering up the front of their white gowns.
