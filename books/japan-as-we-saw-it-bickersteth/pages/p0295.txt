It was indeed a valuable example of what could be done by out-station work—that is, work started by an English Mission having its centre in a large city. Such a Mission if well manned is able to send out missionaries from time to time on evangelistic tours in the neighbouring villages, who, after winning a certain number of converts, can entrust them to the care of a native catechist until the place is ready for a Japanese clergyman in charge. Could the number of these strong missionary centres in Japan be multiplied, it can scarcely be doubted that the result would be most remarkable.

But what was the actual state of the case as brought before us during our visit to the country in 1891? In Tokyo, Osaka, Kobe, Fukuoka, and other places we found hopeful central missions, but all in charge of them were obliged to sorrowfully acknowledge that they had not means or missionaries to overtake their own work, and that if out-station work had been attempted, it had generally been at the sacrifice of still more pressing work in the centre, or during the holidays of the missionaries.
