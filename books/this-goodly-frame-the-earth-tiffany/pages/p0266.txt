The most diverting sight of all is to watch the mother monkeys, their little coal-black babies clinging by all four hands to the fur on the maternal stomach, thus leaving mamma free play of all her limbs for the execution of the most bewildering leaps. For an emergency—say a house a-fire and a natural desire on the maternal part to grab up as many silver spoons as possible before taking flight—monkey babies understand better how to keep out of the way than the most highly evolved of human babies.

I have introduced this monkey episode, and especially the latter part of it, not in a spirit of trifling, but for its serious architectural and, I may add, theological bearing on the immediate object we have in view. We are ascending Mt. Abu to see and interpret the famous Jain temples there. In their infinite elaboration of carvings of figures, animal and human, they present what I might fitly call an opium or hasheesh delirium of symbolism. Nothing stands for what it is, from the wing of a butterfly to the trunk of an elephant, but ever and always as suggestion of some underlying mystic meaning. Fortunately, it had so happened that only a few days before I had been reading the record of a controversy which took place ages ago between two rival Hindu sects.
