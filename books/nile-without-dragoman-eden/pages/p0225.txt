Clothes seem an encumbrance, and one longs for a constant air bath. Curiously, and, perhaps, fortunately for us, the khamsin did not blow once during the month we spent above Philæ. When it does so, Nubia becomes intolerably hot. From the smaller plagues usually found in a hot climate, it is comparatively free. Musquitoes, except at Philæ, we never saw; bugs do not exist, and fleas are scarce, still they are large, veritable lobsters, and in agility or appetite they equal the celebrated <i>pulce di Rimini.</i> The black fly is the single insect nuisance to be dreaded, and from them we rarely suffered. But then we took against this universal household nuisance most severely repressive measures. Morning and evening the flyflap was worked, and every fly that it was possible to reach was remorselessly killed. This may have been cruel, but it was essential for comfort, nay, for health. Flies get so much more to eat on board a dahabeah than on shore that they will not leave it.

The boat, as it passes along the banks, constantly attracts and rarely parts with them. The number on board consequently would increase and increase until one's life became a burden; and the repose necessary for health in a hot climate would be exchanged for an ineffectual defence of one's face and hands.
