The two Russian officers, Moore, the two Caucasian chiefs, the hunter Achia, and Peter Knubel, left the camp at one in the morning for the ascent of Elbruz, the wind being of rather ominous strength, but the night clear. When they got to the edge of the great snow-field they saw that the two peaks of the mountain were veiled by what they took to be a cloud. Hoping that this might lift at dawn they went on, and trudged over the névé for about two hours, at the end of which time the Russian officers could struggle no longer against increasing exhaustion, and were obliged to stop and retreat. It was not the least astonishing that this should happen. Walking up snow-slopes at a great height is most fatiguing work, and constantly produces complete prostration in men unaccustomed to it. The Russian officers, admirable and fearless horsemen, able to keep in the saddle for fourteen or fifteen hours, were anything but practised walkers, and the exertion told on them so severely that at last they became utterly unable to go further on the upward course. They were therefore forced to turn back, while Moore, the Caucasians, and Peter Knubel continued the ascent. But inaccessible to all men was Minghi Tau on that day.
