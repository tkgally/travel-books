Just opposite us, as we came into the valley, was Gelati, the great monastery of the Southern Caucasus. An edifice more admirably placed it would be hard to find. Unlike our own monastic buildings, which are commonly on low ground, Gelati, high up on a magnificently wooded slope, just under a bold crest, seems placed there to rule the beautiful valley below. The white walls and light-green roofs which mark the Greek Church stand out brightly from the dark mass behind, and, being under the ridge and not on it, the monastery has all the dignity which comes from a lofty site without the look of bleakness and exposure belonging to buildings which on a lofty ridge stand out against the sky line. The difficulties of transport must be considerable, but labour is cheap in the country, horses are many, and perhaps the monks of Gelati are not so hard to please as others have been. I believe that, in the Greek Church, a married priest whose wife dies must enter the monastic order. There must therefore be a large number of widowers in a Greek monastery, and men who have been married are notoriously less particular about meat and drink than confirmed bachelors.
