Mathozinhos and Leça are built on those green slopes that curve so gracefully round the little bay; and there, amidst orange and lemon-trees, roses and myrtles and vine-covered bowers, are cottages, <i>quintas,</i> and villas rising one above another. The roofs are bright red, the fronts white and green, or faced with <i>azulejos</i>—china tiles—the prettiest being embossed with white flowers or stars on a pale yellow ground. The deep porches are filled with flowers, and vines or creeping-plants nearly cover some of the roofs. These attractive dwellings vary greatly in size and in the extent of their gardens and grounds; but all are clean and neat, and many of them elegant. On the shore are a few boats; two of them have spread their white sails and are standing out towards the sea. Hanging over the parapet of the stone bridge are two rosy peasant girls; perhaps they are admiring their faces in the clear stream, or more likely they are coquetting with the young boatman who sits beneath the bridge in his boat.

On the summit of the hill stands the church of “Good Jesus,” or Our Lord of Mathozinhos. The legend tells us of the image so named that ages ago it was miraculously cast upon the sands, but with one arm wanting—torn from it by the fury of the sea. Soon after, a poor woman collecting drift-wood amongst the rocks to boil her <i>pot-àu-feu,</i> met with a piece—part of a spar, as it seemed—that answered her purpose exactly.
