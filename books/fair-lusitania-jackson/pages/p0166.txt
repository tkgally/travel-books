The director of the Praça, though himself, perhaps, not so much moved by the affecting scene as he ought to have been, was yet sufficiently considerate of the feelings of others to order the blind musicians to go on with the air which had been broken off on the entrance of <i>El Pôllo.</i> During its continuance the idol and his devotees regained their composure.

A bell now rings. A low chair is brought in and placed in the arena, just opposite to us, but further back than the centre, to allow the bull a longer space for his rush. <i>El Pôllo</i> takes his seat, and his <i>bandarilheiros</i>—one of them his brother, and remarkably like him—hand him a pair of barbed darts, while his <i>capinho</i> throws a little crimson silk cloak over his shoulder.

This pretty fellow is so clever, surely he can be in no danger; yet I hear a tremulous whisper behind me—“Santa Anna, protect him!” Could the prayer be heard, a solemn Amen would resound throughout the building.—The bull has become dreadfully restless in his narrow cell. But the door is opening,—now for the rush! Actually, the creature only thrusts forth his head and glares threateningly on the multitude.
