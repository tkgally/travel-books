The <i>Hespanholas</i>, for the most part, dress here in black, and wear their long veils. They walk up and down the dusty <i>allées</i> with a very stately air, fluttering their fans. Some of them are followed by two or three pretty children, always most elegantly dressed, and attended by perhaps as many picturesque nurses, with red roses, bunches of jasmine, or other natural flowers twined in the massive braids of their jetty hair. A <i>nursery-man</i> accompanies them. He generally wears a brown braided dress, crimson belt, and broad hat. His duties appear to be to carry the little ones, to run after their balls, and to bear submissively the petty tyrannies of his group of fractious <i>niños</i>. At other times he acts as footman, dividing the duty with the <i>senhora’s</i> waiting-maid; and they saunter on together at a short distance behind their mistress, he, whenever he can do so unobserved, taking a few whiffs at a <i>cigarro</i>. At eleven o’clock the last cars leave for Oporto, when there is a general rush for places, and the <i>Praia</i> is partly cleared; but the dwellers at Foz are in no hurry to leave, and often at midnight there are still a few lingerers there.

The evening of the <i>festa</i> was not an inviting one for a stroll, but I walked with my Portuguese friend beyond the <i>Praia</i> to the hill near the castle. There was no moon, and a broad arched band of black cloud spanned the heavens.
