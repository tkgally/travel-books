But they almost outdo them in the display of massive gold chains, some of which are long, and large enough in pattern for a civic dignitary. Besides these adornments, they wear crosses and clasps, earrings, lockets, and rings; gay-coloured chintzes, embroidered kerchiefs and aprons; <i>bouquets</i> and wreaths of flowers in their hats; natty <i>sabots</i> and sometimes stockings.

While waiting for a <i>cicerone</i> to guide us through the labyrinth of narrow, winding streets leading to the <i>Sê velha</i> (the old cathedral), we went into the dining-room of the hotel. It is long and lofty, and has over the mantel-piece an announcement on a large painted board that “No smoking is permitted during dinner when <i>senhoras</i> are present.” A great and unnecessary penance, I fancy, is thus inflicted on the guests, and if students dine there, they will surely be tempted to wish the ladies at the <i>diable</i>—pardon me, I pray you, this nervous expression—for what Spaniard or Portuguese could dine comfortably without smoke? And how few of their <i>senhoras</i> would wish them to do so! Indeed, at a private table, I have seen a <i>senhora</i> enjoy her <i>cigaretto</i> after dinner as much as the gentlemen, and although I do not smoke myself, yet I have become so habituated to being smoked, that I fear I shall miss the odorous fumes of “the weed” when I leave this fair land.
