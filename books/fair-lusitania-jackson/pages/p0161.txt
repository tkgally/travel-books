[[cont]]<i>toirada.</i> A flourish of trumpets, announcing that the director of the <i>corrida</i> was about to enter the tribune, was also the signal for a call—“<i>Abaixo os chapeos do sol</i>,”—down with the umbrellas; and it was readily obeyed.

From a gateway facing the tribune—a projecting box below the royal one—there entered a horseman, bearing a lance, and dressed in a coat of dark green, with deep cuffs and broad lappels. He wore ruffles, frilled shirt and long laced cravat; a three-cornered feathered hat, white breeches, and riding boots to the height of the knee. He was young and good looking, and as, hat in hand, he gracefully ambled his horse round the arena, bowing to the assembled throng, and especially low to the <i>senhoras</i>—who were in full force in the <i>cadeiras</i> or stalls—his whole appearance was that which a romantic fancy might picture to itself a Paul Clifford’s, or other fascinating highwayman of the olden time.

Accompanying this captivating <i>cavalleiro,</i> and also making their best bow to the company, were the <i>Matador de Espada</i> and six <i>bandarilheiros</i> and <i>capinhos,</i> who attack the bull on foot. Following them were the <i>moços de forcado,</i> who seize the bull in front by the horns, and jump between them upon his head; and lastly came the <i>andarilhos</i>—little boys, apparently apprentice bull-fighters, whose office seems to be to scamper about distributing darts, picking up broken ones, or any stray fallen <i>capas.</i> From this “<i>magestoso cortijo,</i>” as the programme termed it, stepped forward a pace or two, the <i>Espada, El Pôllo</i>—and a remarkably pretty fellow, indeed, he proved to be.
