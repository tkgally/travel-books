The eyes of the horse he rode on this occasion were not bandaged. It was a pretty, graceful animal, and pranced and curveted as if rather pleased than frightened at the contest before him. The bull had been most noisy and obstreperous before he was let out, and unlike the others, which seemed rather to avoid the horse, this bull attacked him as soon as he entered. Twice he had been turned aside by the <i>cavalleiro’s farpas,</i> or lances. But he was a wily, cunning creature, and appeared to watch an opportunity of driving horse and rider against the <i>trincheira</i>, when injury might have resulted to both.

I really believe that the bull, as he stood eyeing them askance, was measuring accurately the distance between them; for with a sudden and tremendous rush he darted across the arena. That he would stop them ere they could pass seemed inevitable. But contact was avoided, and the bull foiled by the horse easily and gracefully leaping over him. And the <i>cavalleiro</i> not only did not swerve in his seat, but contrived as he passed to plant another <i>farpa</i> in his antagonist’s neck! You must imagine the plaudits. They had not nearly subsided when we left; and it would have been a great offence to show any impatience to leave, or desire to turn one’s back on them. But there was little to follow, and that, after such a feat, could possess very scant interest.
