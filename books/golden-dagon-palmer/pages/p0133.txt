That afternoon the Purser and I landed at Kemmendine with a watering party—the village seemingly deserted, and picturesquely desolate. A few rods from the landing brought us to the one long, narrow street, of dirty, low-eaved bamboo huts, which, following the line of the shore, was floored with little black bricks, and roofed with multitudinous and various foliage, downhanging and dense. A pariah dog or two, skulking and spy-like; a forgotten cat or two, singed and sorry-looking; one famished mina, screaming from a cage, were all the inhabitants.

Two or three hundred yards from the landing, to the left, in the middle of the village and of the pavement, was the well from which our men filled their casks. To the right, and much nearer, was a shadowy banian grove, through which a whispering alley led up to a temple, many pinnacled and grotesquely carved, wherein was an ampitheatre of shelves, piled high with images—little and big, of wood and stone, and lead and plaster—of the Stagnant Calm. A rude paling inclosed the whole, and on the right was a thicket of thorny underbush.
