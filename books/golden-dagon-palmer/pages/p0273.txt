The Burmese, who were arrant cowards, would creep cautiously forward, shoot, and run—firing very hastily their wretched muskets, with the most uncertain aim of the worst of all possible marksmen; so that, while our shots were never wasted, theirs had, as yet, done no harm. In a little while, however, one of our men received a ball in his shoulder—a flesh-wound merely, from which he soon recovered, but which disabled him for the time.

Soon after that, my countryman—he who had come to my rescue when I was sinking—loaded his musket under the awning, occupied, in his turn, the bow of the boat, which had just been vacated by another, and taking deliberate aim at a Burman—who, in that ridiculous spirit of bravado, so familiar to all who have had dealings with the tattooed rascals, was performing a war-dance, making grimaces, and gesticulating with all sorts of insulting antics—shot him dead. Immediately a dozen muskets replied—fired straight at him.
