The crushing-mill was turned by bullocks, about twelve being harnessed to it at once. The juice was conveyed from the mill to the boiling-house, where it was converted into sugar; and behind, a still was in operation making rum. The cane, after the juice is pressed from it, is called <i>trash</i>, and is stacked in a house and kept dry, and is then used as fuel to boil the sugar,—and so we saw the whole process. There seemed to be no want of labour just here, but the negroes, when they work at all, do but little: they go into the field at six o’clock and leave it at ten, considering that that is a day’s work, and at least two days in the week they do nothing at all,—one being market-day, is quite a sufficient excuse for idleness.

This indolence entirely prevents the culture of cotton, which might be produced to any extent in Jamaica: but cotton must be picked exactly when it is ripe, whereas sugar may wait for a week or two without deteriorating, and a shower of rain does it no harm, but rather the contrary.
