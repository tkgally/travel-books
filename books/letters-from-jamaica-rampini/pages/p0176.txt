The drive between Bluefields and Savanna-la-Mar, the chief town of Westmoreland, was, at least for the first half the way, exceedingly beautiful. We passed two or three picturesque little fishing villages, burrowing under over-hanging cliffs, or buried under broad-leaved plantains and bananas. From the honeycombed rocks by the seashore hung long withes with flat glossy leaves. In the yard of one of the negro huts we noticed a large Jaca-tree (<i>Artocarpus integrifolia</i>) whose strange noduled fruit, which weighs sometimes sixty or seventy pounds, grows out of its stem. Close by, twining round a cotton tree, whose sap and substance it had utterly destroyed, was a wild fig (<i>Ficus Indica</i>); a state of things which in Jamaica is taken as a type of “the Scotchman hugging the Creole to death.” Near by it, depended a lovely creeper with brown velvety pods, whose tempting clusters we were about to pluck, when we were suddenly arrested by a loud exclamation from Bob.

“Cracious!” cried he, “if you touch dis ting, I don’t know what I will have to do with you. I will hab to get hot hashes” (he meant ashes) “an’ put you into dem.”
