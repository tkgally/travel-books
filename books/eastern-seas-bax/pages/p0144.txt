After deciding that we were friends this party also fired off their matchlocks in the air. Soon after passing them we came to the camp of the Peppo-hoans, which was composed of huts fenced round with a stockade of bamboo, and built on the bank of a stream. Many savages were living in the camp with one of their chiefs who spoke Chinese; some of these gave us cakes of rice and oranges as an expression of their friendship for us. Altogether in the camp, including Peppo-hoans, savages and ourselves, there were about eighty assembled. A hut was given to Mr. M. and myself and our servants, but being badly made of coarse grass did not keep out either wind or rain.

The young savages appeared frank and pleasant people, but the old ones had a very suspicious and cunning look, which, I suppose, must have been caused by the constant state of harassing warfare they live in with the Chinese and neighbouring savage tribes.
