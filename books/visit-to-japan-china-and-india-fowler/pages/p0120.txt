A crowded ship has discomforts under the most favourable circumstances, but these unfortunate emigrants were packed together as closely as possible; and when the owners and captains of the ships were actuated simply by the desire to make as much money as possible out of their human cargo, no wonder if the sufferings were horrible. The Chinese coolie traffic vied with the African slave-trade in its abominations. Men were decoyed from their homes, made to sign agreements in a language which they did not understand, forced on shipboard, and sent to a distant land never to return. It would be a horrible lot for any man, and for a Chinaman, with his intense desire to lay his bones in his native land, it has additional horrors. Attention was called to the trade in 1871, by the case of the “Dolores Ugarte,” a vessel which sailed from Macao for Peru, on May 15th, with a cargo of 656 coolies. On the 17th, a fire having broken out, the hatchways were fastened down, the crew took to the boats, and 600 Chinese were left to their fate.
