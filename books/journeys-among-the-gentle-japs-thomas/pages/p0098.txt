[[cont]]Tramways were laid along the main streets in 1882, electric light was introduced into the city three or four years later, and in 1890 a telephone exchange was established in it, and an electrical railway laid in one of its great public parks.

Such, in brief, has been the history of Tôkyô. That of Yedo “for the most part consists of a succession of earthquakes, fires, typhoons, epidemics, floods, and droughts” (<i>Murray</i>). In the year 1703 no less than 37,000 people are said to have lost their lives by an earthquake. An epidemic in 1773 carried off 190,000 persons. The last great earthquake occurred in 1855, when the loss of life was immense.

A great part of Tôkyô has been recovered from the sea during the last three centuries. This would include Tsukiji, the foreign concession, where—at the Métropole, situated on the Bund—I was quartered during the first few days of my stay at the capital. Other hotels in European style are the Imperial, the Tôkyô, and the Seiyo-ken.
