This has given rise to a proverb—a Japanese rendering of the proverb that “even Homer nods”—<i>Kôbô mo fude no ayamari</i> (“Even Kôbô sometimes wrote wrong”).

But after all there is nothing miraculous about the inscription in the Daiya-gawa, as there is authority for attributing it to a disciple of Jigen Daishi, only two centuries ago.

I set out from Nikkô with a pair of <i>waraji</i> (straw sandals), which are said to give a better foothold in walking along a mountainous road. They were worn tied underneath my boots, for which I had a difficulty in procuring a pair big enough, as the natives, male as well as female, have such dainty feet. In the presence of a crowd of curious onlookers, the obliging and polite little shopman who dealt in straw articles at Nikkô was at infinite pains to secure to the soles of my heavy boots the largest pair he had in stock, but, alas! I had scarcely got clear of the village when, without a warning, they flew off. Such was my first and last experience of <i>waraji</i>, which are recommended to Europeans as excellent foot-gear, but which I found of very little service. The pair cost me a farthing.
