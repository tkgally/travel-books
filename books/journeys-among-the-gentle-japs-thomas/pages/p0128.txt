Arriving at the temple, we come, at the bottom of a stone stairway, upon a pool of clear water, fed by a small stream flowing through the mouth of a brass dragon. The spring was miraculously called into being by Jikaku Daishi, the founder of the temple, and a famous Buddhist abbot of the ninth century. A religious exercise—not unknown in other parts of Japan—is to stand naked, save the loin-cloth, under the stream for several hours, the effect of it being to wash away all sinful taint. That penitential lustration is called <i>Sui-Giyo</i>, or “water-cure” for sin, and it is to be suspected that many a light-hearted Jap exposes his naked body to the cooling stream in the warm weather from quite another motive than penitence. Many, on the other hand, are genuine devotees, and the self-sacrifice of standing beneath the stream on a bitterly cold day in mid-winter, as is often done, argues no little faith. Another religious exercise which is performed in the same precincts is the <i>Hiyakudo</i>, or “the hundred times,” which consists in passing backwards and forwards between two points, repeating a prayer each time.
