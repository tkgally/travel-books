[[cont]]I thought that we should probably find at or near the houses of the missionaries the usual native who “knows English,” but such was not in evidence.

After looking up the missionaries immediately on my arrival and finding them not at home, I was taken by my coolie to the <i>Wayo-tei</i> restaurant—a beautifully fitted-up house of a kind rare out of Tôkyô. I was greeted by the entire <i>personnel</i> of the house with the usual prostrations and a chorus of <i>Ohayos</i>, and <i>O ide nasai</i> (“Condescend to make your honourable entrance”) from the cheery little hostess, and, after doffing my footgear, was conducted by a pretty, tripping <i>mousmé</i> up a shining stairway of polished cedar and pine into a long airy apartment, exquisitely panelled and matted. In Japan, I may observe here, there are three sorts of houses which accommodate travellers, namely, the <i>yadoya</i> (inn), <i>ryôri-ya</i> (restaurant), and <i>chaya</i> (tea-house). The first alone provides sleeping accommodation. The <i>ryôri-ya</i> prepares meals with less delay than the <i>yadoya</i>, while the <i>chaya</i> is a house which only provides light refreshment, such as tea and sweets. Often, however, inns are included under the general denomination of tea-houses.
