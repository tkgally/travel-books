In the ninth century it made rapid progress under the teaching of Kôbô Daishi, its greatest saint and apostle, who had spent some time in China studying its tenets. He is credited with the invention of the <i>Hiragana</i> syllabary, and has thus been the chief means of disseminating the Buddhist scriptures throughout Japan. He taught that the native Shintô deities were <i>avatars,</i> or incarnations, of Buddhist deities, and thus made it possible for those Shintôists who became converts to the imported religion to preserve to some extent their ancient faith.

Japanese Buddhism is split up into various sects and sub-sects, of which the chief are the <i>Tendai</i> and the <i>Shingon</i> (which are of Chinese origin), the <i>Jôdo, Nichiren,</i> and <i>Shin.</i> They hold widely different views upon the doctrine of Nirvana, some believing in the utter annihilation of the soul, others in its separate existence, but as part of the divine. But, according to Sir E. Satow, the points in dispute between them are highly metaphysical, the principles of one sect being considered incomprehensible except to such as have attained to Buddhahood.
