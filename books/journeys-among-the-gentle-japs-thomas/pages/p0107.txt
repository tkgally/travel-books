The following is a specimen of “English as she is spoke” in Japan, copied from a public notice which caught my eye in the grounds of Asakusa.

<blockquote>
[[center]] “NOTICE!
“A wonderful show of the blood-stained garments, letters, and many other things from our officers and soldiers on the battlefield.
“The exactly resembling portraits and pictures of our military and navy excellent officers and soldiers.
“The dreadful views of battlefield with oil-painted pictures.
[[center]] “Admission free.”
</blockquote>

An English notice forbidding trespassing or any other illegal act is often headed, “Kind notice,” or “Very kind notice”—another proof of the innate politeness and gentleness of the people.

The Imperial Palace at Tôkyô is not open to the public, but its approaches are accessible, and are very imposing. It occupies the sight of the old castle and grounds of the Shôguns, and has been inhabited by the present Emperor since 1889. The park is enclosed by a wide moat, which, with its lotus flowers and water lilies, and myriads of wild fowl, is one of the prettiest sights in the city. The encircling wall is of enormous Cyclopean masonry.
