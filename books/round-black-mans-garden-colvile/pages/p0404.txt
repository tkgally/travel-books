Passing through a broad arched gateway, we entered a high-walled enclosure some two hundred yards square, in one corner of which stood a well-built European-looking house giving on to a covered courtyard. Going up a broad flight of wooden steps, we were ushered into the dining-room, a nicely decorated apartment, whose most prominent feature was a large coloured photograph of our host, which had been enlarged from an amateur’s negative sent to England for the purpose, in which the large coral bead that he always wore was done full justice to.

After a short time a little slave came in with two dishes—on one a substantial piece of roast-meat, on the other palm-oil “chop”—quite the most delicious mixture that I had ever tasted of shrimps stewed in palm-oil, with just a pinch of ground chillies. It was so good that I have often regretted that by the time palm-oil reaches England it has lost its freshness; and although doubtless excellent for the purpose for which it is imported—the manufacture of soap, and the bright-coloured but rather unsavoury-smelling grease which is applied to railway-carriage wheels—it is no longer suitable for culinary purposes.
