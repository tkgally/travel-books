Several of them are tied by the horns to a rope, which is fastened to spars placed athwart the canoes; they are then driven into the sea with many shrieks and blows from the natives, and have to swim for dear life, towed by the canoes, to the ship which is to convey them. When they arrive at the ship’s side, a rope is passed under them, and they are hoisted on deck. It seemed terribly cruel, for I am told they do not even use a rope thick enough to prevent its cutting into them. I saw one clever animal get himself free of the ropes and swim to the reefs, where he seemed perfectly happy, stumbling about out of reach of his persecutors.

A great many people came on board from the town; among them was Monsieur Baissade, the doctor belonging to the French Residency, to whom we were introduced, and under whose care we were placed for the journey up country. Soon after anchoring, our Captain came up to us accompanied by an English officer, who had come from H.M.S. “Penguin” for their mails, and who kindly invited us to lunch on board. On arriving there, however, we were introduced to the Captain—King Hall—who asked us to lunch with him. On sitting down I was surprised to find that my neighbour, the first Lieutenant, was Mr Stanhope, whom I had not seen since we were both little children at Pau; while Harry’s last meeting with him was at Dongola.
