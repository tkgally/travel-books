While I was thinking of all this, its incongruity with its surroundings was sharply brought to my notice by a splash and a sudden bump, both due to the remarkable thoroughfare by which alone the metropolis can be reached. This consists of a track slightly raised in the centre, some fifteen inches wide, and occupying the whole ridge of one of the little mud-banks already mentioned. With the ground as wet as it was, I am not at all sure if I could have walked along it alone; but the extraordinary feats that my bearers had performed in this way, made me take it as a matter of course that they would run along it, leaning outwards like a V. Perhaps the excitement of nearing the capital was too much for them, but whatever the cause, the two “wheelers” suddenly slipped, and, gliding into the neighbouring rice-fields, left me sitting—feet in air—on the ground. It was a good job it was not the “leaders” who had slipped, or I should probably have had an opportunity of seeing whether I admired the capital more in an inverted position.

The part of the ridge for which we made is ascended by a narrow path cut out of the solid rock, and polished by centuries of barefooted traffic. No attempt has been made, by zigzags or other devices, to lessen the incline, and it would have been a steepish climb even had the road afforded good foothold: as it was, I should think it absolutely impassable to any one wearing boots.
