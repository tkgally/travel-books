Half the road of the past day’s march had been over fallen trees and slippery logs across the swamps and <i>cienegas</i>; but we had got over a greater distance than the ordinary <i>jornadas</i> of our peons, although we had completed the day’s work at half-past four o’clock. The prospect of reaching human habitations again, and of such an interesting people as the Jívaros, had made us push on faster than usual.

The house to which we bent our steps was a large one belonging to an old Jívaro named Namúco, whom we found at home with some nine or ten other men and women who were living in the same house. We were well received, our men immediately on arriving having represented us to the Indians as <i>jatun apos</i> (important personages), with offers of hot roasted <i>yuca</i> and boiled plantains and fish, prepared in the favourite Indian manner, namely, wrapped in plantain leaves and steamed with a small quantity of water—a method far superior to boiling. Very acceptable and delicious did we find this meal, which we could set upon at once without the usual toil of pitching the tent, gathering firewood, unpacking utensils and cooking our own food.
