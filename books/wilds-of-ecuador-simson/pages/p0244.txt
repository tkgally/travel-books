I therefore sent for the Piojé and the boy; but a message was returned to me that Firmin was away in the woods with them both. An evasion like that, however, did not serve him, for, in response, I seized his canoe, and after making it fast alongside, told his messenger to inform him, that unless he sent me the two persons I demanded, at once, I would take Firmin himself back to Brazil. As he was a Brazilian deserter, the threat told, and the Piojé and the boy were sent on board, so that we were enabled to continue our voyage to the place where the Orejones lived, and where wood-cutting was again resumed. This operation generally occupied four days at each station, and may here be described. We usually cut about 1,100 to 1,200 split logs for the launch, and left from 3,000 to 3,500 stacked for the larger steamer. The first step was to find a “<i>loma</i>” or hillock, which are few and far between, for it is only in the old forest on high ground that hard woods are plentiful, with the exception of one wood in certain places, “Palo Mulato,” or “Capirona,” which is very hard, although it grows in low places in newer portions of the forest. Soft woods are naturally of little use for steaming purposes, whilst the hard ones burn almost as well as coal, and can be used green.
