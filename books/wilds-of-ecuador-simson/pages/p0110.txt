As yesterday, the track continued along the top of one of those most curious geological formations called <i>cuchillas</i> (knives). The name is fitting, for one may well liken them among hills to the upturned blades of knives. The <i>cuchilla</i> is a ridge, generally running for a considerable distance, sometimes ten, and even fifteen miles, uninterruptedly, with varying elevation up to as much as perhaps 500 feet from the base. On one side they are generally almost perpendicular, and on the other descend at a sharp angle. The most wonderful part of their construction is their composition, which consists of loose vegetable earth and loam. It was to me a subject of marvel how these blades could be held aloft solely by the support of the entangled roots and vegetation with which they are covered. And this covering is not only the prop of the structure, but also the safeguard of the traveller; for in many places where the blade is only two or three feet broad, it would be far too hazardous to file along it, were not an effective balustrade furnished by the friendly trees, bushes and creepers, which also hide the yawning of the abyss from the eye, and divest the depth of its unsteadying influence.
