[[cont]]and even in private trade prices and conditions were laid down by law, leaving no exercise to the commercial ingenuity of the individual. Another writer,[[fnref *]] who has searched through all the old authentic records of the Cabildo at Quito, tells us that “immediately after the foundation of Quito, the Cabildo commenced to regulate all the affairs cf daily life. The charges of tavern-keepers were fixed by law, without any reference to the fluctuations in the market price of provisions. Bakers were ordered to give thirty-five pounds of bread for one <i>peso</i> of gold, under penalty of ten <i>pesos.</i> It was declared an offence to charge more for a <i>fanega</i> of wheat than two <i>pesos,</i> or one <i>peso</i> for the <i>fanega</i> of Indian corn. The shoemakers were commanded to make shoes at two <i>pesos</i> two <i>tomines</i> the pair, and boots at four <i>pesos.</i> In 1537, the prices of blacksmith work were regulated by law, and the transgressors threatened with heavy penalties. It was declared unlawful to leave the city without a permit or order from the Lieutenant-Governor; offenders to be fined fifty <i>pesos.</i> Persons who were out of Quito during the Christmas or Easter festivals incurred a fine of ten <i>pesos.</i> Under penalty of death and confiscation of property, it was ordained that nobody should barter or buy gold, silver, or pearls from an Indian, except in the presence of the royal <i>veedor</i> (superintendent), whose duty it was to see that the transaction was not against the will of the Indian, and that the king was not defrauded of his fifth.
