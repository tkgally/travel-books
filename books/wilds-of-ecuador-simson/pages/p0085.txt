The passenger sits in the loop, and is pulled dangling across the stream, or works himself across hand over hand. Cargo is firmly tied in the same position, and hauled from side to side by a thinner cord attached to the fork. Of course to pass thirty persons and an almost equal number of loads, one by one and securely fastened, is almost a day’s work; so we saw it was useless to attempt to cross that day before dark. The tent was therefore pitched on the wet, mossy and muddy bank, rain pouring down heavily since we had got half-way from Santa Inez to these inhospitable shores. The river was much higher than we had seen it on our previous visits, and by the ordinary method we should not have been able to pass it, <i>i.e.</i> by throwing trunks of trees across from rock to rock.

A few words must be dedicated to the Topo, as it is the most notorious affluent of the Upper Pastassa, and has caused trouble and danger to many. It forms the most dreaded barrier between the east and the west; and is truly the Rubicon of Ecuador for travellers to the “Provincia del Oriente.” For the Napo, and even for Canelos, traders mostly descend <i>viâ</i> Quito and Papallacta, the only other available route, solely to avoid this torrent; though on that road is the Cosanga river, not much less to be feared.
