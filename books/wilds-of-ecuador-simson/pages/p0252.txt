The next station which I had fixed, the River Salado Grande, was reached, and I calculated it to be about 130 miles from the Orejones. Its name is no doubt derived from the proximity somewhere of a “salado,” or saltlick, where all the animals of the forest congregate to lick up the brackish water with which the soil is impregnated, or which oozes from the ground in springs. In other localities I visited two such saltlicks, and it was a sight to behold to see the endless tracks of animals which must frequent such spots at certain times. Round one of the “salados” I went to, the whole soil of the forest was churned into deep mire for a space nearly a mile in diameter; the undergrowth was entirely eradicated, many of the smaller trees torn down and trodden under foot, and the roots of the large trees to a great extent laid bare, whilst the whole ground bore the appearance of having been overrun by dense herds of peccari, tapir, deer, jaguar, and other smaller animals.

After four days’ stay at the Salado river, where we took in our usual supply of wood for fuel and left a stock for the larger steamer, we tied a letter, enveloped in large waterproof leaves, to a cross, giving an account of the journey and the channels of the river, and set out again up-stream.
