Under the circumstances we were compelled to assure him, without going into further details, that although all the Indians had fled, there was no disease among us; so, without even waiting ten minutes for rest after his thirteen days’ foot journey, he again set out, with a couple of boiled cobs of Indian corn in his hand to eat on the way, reached his friends again the same night, and reappeared with nearly the whole horde, men, women and children, the next day.

The <i>padre</i> and Mr. Sarkady had just left for Santa Rosa with the “<i>curaca</i>” and two or three other Indians who had at last also arrived to redeem our prisoner, leaving me to arrange matters with the Záparos and Llore, whom, after considerable difficulty and persuasion, we had induced to accompany us to Iquitos. He was a most valuable acquisition to our party, as one of the few men who knew the entire river well, having spent two or three years roving about its course, chiefly exploring for indiarubber. He was moreover conversant with the ways of the Záparos—relatives of his mother-in-law—their language, and also Quíchua; besides being a most expert hunter, and a canoeman such as only are those of the Esmeraldas river, on the west coast, whence he hailed. We could not have secured a better companion, or one more able to furnish us with correct information.
