There is no pier or inner harbour at Reykjavik, only some wooden jetties, but usually there is a fair amount of shipping in the fine roadstead. We were soon surrounded by small boats, and people glad to welcome us in safety; for there had been a gale in Iceland heavier than any that had been experienced since midwinter—most unwonted weather for June. Bad passages are, however, like bad dreams when one awakes, they are forgotten on arrival, or, at least, not remembered so as to deter people from going to sea again. We landed,—and I may mention as characteristic, that as our boatman had to carry our numerous packages a long way up to our lodging, I paid the boy what seemed to me about enough; but meeting the man a few days later, he insisted on returning to me one-third of the fee, as it was too much. The next agreeable trait which met us was the hospitality. We were asked everywhere to coffee, to dinner, to tea, both by people to whom we had introductions and others, and we were never a day on our own resources all the first week we spent at Reykjavik. But such things belong to pre-guide-book days, and will vanish before the advance of the tourist.
