“Well, have you heard what your husband and brother have been about? They have had a fight with the robbers, and cleaned them out.”

Pleasant news indeed for us! For we had received letters every day saying that they were prospering, and having an excellent journey, without a word of robbers. It appeared, however, that this afternoon’s coach from Toluca had brought word from the diligencia authorities at Maravatio or Morelia, to the chief of the diligencias here, that some of “<i>los Señores Caballeros</i>” had been attacked by robbers, killed several, and driven on unhurt themselves; which last item set our minds a little at rest.

A few days later I received the following letter from my brother, which may serve to give some idea of the chances of the road in Mexico:—

[[right]]“<sc>Salamanca</sc>, <i>June</i> 10.

“<sc>My dear</sc> * * *,—. . . We left Maravatio in the diligence at 3.30 <sc>a.m.</sc>, the Governor inside, Gen. P. and I on the box. Of course it had been raining, and was horridly dark, so we kept a good look-out. About five miles out of town, we ran down into a gully, with a stone wall on the left, and high tree cactus to the right. Suddenly from a break in the wall, and from behind a grove of cacti, out sally—without speaking, and at a walk—five men on horseback. It was the queerest sight—it seems a dream now.

“A ghostly figure walks across the horses’ heads; they stop: we have a vague feeling of uncertainty what to do; when the sharp whisper of the cochero breaks the spell—‘<i>Ahi viene! ahi viene!</i>’ here they come!

“The next thing I remember was taking a cool shot at a man about ten yards from me, while Gen. P.’s pistol was cracking right and left, blinding me with smoke. Alas!
