The stream is full of excellent trout, and we tried to get some from a family of Germans who have taken up a claim right in the middle of the Park: but being surly, disagreeable people, and possibly feeling they had no right to be there, they would give us neither fish, milk, or butter, though their cows were feeding on Dr. B.’s hay. We felt rather like the man Governor H. tells of, who, travelling in Arkansas, rides up late one day to a lonely ranche. The sole occupant is a woman, who comes to the door hearing a traveller arrive; and the following dialogue ensues:—

<i>Man.</i>—“Good day, ma’am, how goes? Can you give my horse a feed?”

<i>Woman.</i>—“Wa’al, I hain’t got <i>much.</i>”

<i>Man.</i>—“Can you give him some corn and hay?”

<i>Woman.</i>—“Wa’al, I hain’t got no corn, and I hain’t got no hay.”

<i>Man</i> (inquisitively).—“Can you give him some fodder?”

<i>Woman</i> (reflectively).—“Wa’al—no; I hain’t got no fodder.”

The horse must take care of himself evidently; so the traveller begins again persuasively:—

“Well, can you give <i>me</i> something to eat?”

<i>Woman</i> (doubtfully).—“Wa’al, neaow, I guess I hain’t got much.”

<i>Man</i> (impatiently).—“Can you give me some bread and beans?”

<i>Woman.</i>—“Wa’al, I hain’t got no bread, and I hain’t got no beans.”

<i>Man</i> (furiously).—“Why, how <i>do</i> you people do down here?”

<i>Woman</i> (cheerfully).—“Oh, pretty well, thank you; heaow do <i>yeu</i> do?”

So after resting the ponies and making a sketch, we turned homeward, reaching Glenwood at 1 <sc>p.m.</sc>
