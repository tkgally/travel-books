The country was totally uninhabited and indeed totally unfit for habitation, at least the greater part of it. Every now and then we would come across a small flat plain some miles square with a nice rich soil and without a boulder upon it. These grass-covered plains were generally swarming with game, and formed a sort of oasis, as it were, in the middle of these stony deserts. But then the rough country would begin again and our troubles also. I can best describe the general appearance of the country we were crossing by comparing it to a rocky sea-shore at low tide, intersected by deep ravines at frequent intervals. But it was the long thick grass growing up between the rocks and stones that added more than anything else to our difficulties in marching through this rocky district.

The peaked mountain on which we had taken our bearings was now about thirty miles distant due south, and the mountain slightly to the east which we afterwards found out really was Mount Naita, was about thirty miles beyond this peak. We named this new mountain “Central Peak,” as it rises in the centre of the plateau.
