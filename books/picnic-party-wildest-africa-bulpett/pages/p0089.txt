Mrs. McMillan, whom some undeferential person named “The Nuisance” just by way of contrariety because she was the very reverse of a nuisance, enjoyed the sport with the rest of us. She would get up before sunrise and shoot her tiang or water-buck, and return to our mid-day meal as strong and as well as the best of us. They were very pleasant days, those mornings of sport, and afternoons of siesta. The morning call would be just before daylight. A hasty toilet, and there was breakfast ready before one’s tent, just in the half light before the rising of the sun. The breakfast usually consisted of hot coffee and preserved milk, porridge and tinned bacon. This “menu” did not take many minutes to consume, and before the sun was above the horizon we were invariably away from the camp and marching across the flat grassy plain. We were obliged to go mostly on foot, as our mule contingent, which was to come down to us from Abyssinia, had not yet arrived. We had, however, four fine Khartoum donkeys, of which we made the most. By ten o’clock we had generally shot what we wanted, and were on our way back to camp, usually arriving there about twelve or one o’clock, to spend the afternoon in a long chair, waiting and longing for the cool of the evening to come; for the weather was intensely hot.
