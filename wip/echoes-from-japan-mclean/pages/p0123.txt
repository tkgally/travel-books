[[cont]]in the year 1874. At this date he was brought in contact with the special Japanese Embassy, visiting America and Europe for the first time. He was invited to accompany them in the capacity of interpreter, an offer which he accepted. At a later date he was offered a good position in the Government, but declined it. After this he returned to Japan, and selected the ancient capital, Kyoto, as his field of action. He appealed to the authorities for a site on which to erect a college for imparting to the ambitious youths of Japan a thorough knowledge of English, and specific branches of Western science. His Government was glad to accede to his wishes—doubtless, the friends whom he accompanied to Europe remembered that he had a right spirit in him, and that he had refused the lucrative position offered him. The site was immediately marked out, and the college was built at the expense of friends he had made in the United States. He next appealed to the Board of Foreign Missions for fully qualified professors of Christian zeal. They came with the understanding that they were to teach useful branches of knowledge, such as <i>medicine and natural philosophy;</i> and on giving five or six hours daily (Saturdays and Sundays excepted) to secular instruction, they were left free to teach anything else they wished. Of course this opened at once a wide door in the Gospel to the missionary-teachers, and God gave them and Mr. Neejima great success. Most of the students attended the Gospel services and Sunday-schools.
