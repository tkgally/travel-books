‘The Cup belongs to him that strikes it down; and I have seen <i>Sha-Sefi</i>, Grandfather of the present King, in five Courses strike down three Cups. . . .

‘From the Pole to another <i>Mosquee</i>, to the South, just again the Sun-Dial, is the place for all the Poulterers. The rest of the Piazza toward the Palace, is always kept clean, without any Shops, because the King comes often abroad in the Evening to see Lions, Bears, Bulls, Rams, Cocks, and all other sort of Creatures fight, which are brought thither. . . .

‘There are a sort of Tumblers also, that after Dinner set up their Stages in the <i>Meydan</i>, and toward the Evening, they that play the Maid-Marians come and encompass a square place with a course piece of Calicut; and then through another very fine Cloth, the Wenches shew a thousand tumbling Tricks and antick Postures. When they have done, they come and ask the Spectators for Money, who give them every one what they think fit.’

Just across the river is Julfa, the city of the Armenians. Lord Curzon, in his account of this place and by the quotations he makes regarding it, contrives to leave, I think, a rather too unfavourable impression of its character and of its inhabitants. Certainly the place is, as he says, ‘cribbed, cabined, and confined.’ But the picture his words leave behind of a series of slums frequented by drunken men and drabs is scarcely a fair one.
