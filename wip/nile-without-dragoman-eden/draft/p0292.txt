It was 2 p.m. on the next day before the north wind moderated sufficiently to enable us to start, and at 7.30 we reached Benesoueff, a distance of six or seven miles. But our day’s work was not over. The Benesoueff we left going up had a broad navigable river before it, and we anchored under the shade of its trees. Now a huge sandbank, many feet above the water level, and as many hundred yards in breadth, forbade approach to the town. As the situation was exposed, and the weather unsettled, we tried to drop to the foot of the shoals and reach the true bank. We asked our way from the sailors of a number of native lighter boats, but, in spite of their directions, we struck the ground almost immediately. The water was only just too thin for the dahabeah, and the stream, first sweeping her stern round, carried her broadside for more than fifty yards over the bank, grinding and scraping with a noise as of a rush of wind, and a rasping jar that shook every timber. At last we struck. The crew went overboard, and heaved and strained for three hours to force the boat over the shoal into the deeper water beyond. Every foot of progress was registered by a scrape. Now she stuck fast, now we dragged on a few yards, then hung again, and at last, with a long grind, found ourselves afloat.
