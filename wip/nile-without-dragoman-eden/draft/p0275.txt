So we pushed off with the man, and were followed down the bank by the crowd. Sum after sum was offered in redemption of the writ. At last the reis accepted some offer I did not understand, and getting into the felucca, he paddled off to surrender the debtor and receive his due. When he had got half-way to shore, a general cry was raised from the dahabeah. Our men were afraid that as soon as the reis landed the delinquent would escape, and he be beaten. The suggestion of this possibility brought the reis back to us, and I offered to allow the dahabeah to go to shore. This would have given trouble, and was refused. Then I said “Send some one with the reis.” “Yes,” said they all, and “No, not I,” said each individual. The others then suggested that Mohammed, the old man’s brother-in-law, should go, and the man refused. Then Abed went. The Arab character is not courageous, nor altogether nice. Kind to excess as the reis was to our crew, there was only one of them who would run any risk to save him from a beating. But risk there was none, and the party, less the debtor, soon returned with two pieces of silver added to the coppers.

We slept at Sohag, and started next morning before I was up. Waking, I heard a tap on the outside of my window. “What is it?” The language on board the <i>Lotus</i> was peculiar, and the answer given by our interpreter, Ibrahim, was, “<i>Girghis voglio stop.</i>” “<i>Perche?</i>”
