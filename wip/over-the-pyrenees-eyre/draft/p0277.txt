I never saw such good children as those two were. They went half-way to Madrid; and in all that long, weary journey, they never cried or annoyed anyone. I envied the pretty peasant her baby—I, who had nothing better than a <i>perro!</i>

At one station, three or four wild banditti-looking men got into the carriage.

‘Gendarme di Espagna,’ cried one of my neighbours.

‘To prevent the brigands putting their hands in your pocket,’ said one of the new comers.

‘What!’ said I, horrified; ‘do the brigands attack railway travellers?’

Everyone laughed, and tried to mystify me; but the fact was, the <i>gendarmes</i> were only travelling from one station to another, at which they got out. They were fine, powerful men, as most of the Spaniards are. Their costume was very picturesque, but very unsoldierlike, to English eyes.
