Travelling by packet-boat with his family, I think up the Rhine, he noticed that every lady who entered tore her dress against a nail in the boat. ‘Why do you not remove that nail?’ asked he. ‘Oh, they had not had time—they should do it by-and-by.’ Mr. Howitt quietly went to a place where he had observed some carpenter’s tools lying, got them, and extracted the nail. ‘You are an Englishman?’ said a gentleman, coming up to him. ‘Yes,’ answered Mr. Howitt; ‘but how did you know that?’ ‘Oh, easily enough. You took out the nail at once; the Germans would have left it there to tear everybody’s clothes for the next six months, and always said they were going to take it out by-and-by, when they had time.’ It is this promptitude, this doing at once that which ought to be done, this instinctive sense of duty, which is above all things the merit and the characteristic of an Englishman.
