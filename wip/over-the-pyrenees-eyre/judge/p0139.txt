When night came, he slunk away—the Andorrans did not want his life; they had avenged the insult offered to their daughters and sweethearts; he escaped to St. Columba—he dared not return to Escaldos—and thence to France, but he dared never show himself in the valley again.’

Mr. Erskine Murray, in his interesting work on the Pyrenees, lauds the pure manners of the peasants of Andorre; had he lived some months among them, like M. Poéte, he would have learnt that they are by no means the highly moral people he supposed.

‘The women are slaves. They have no voice in disposing of themselves, but are given in marriage by their family, like the French girls; but without being, like the French girls, educated in the seclusion of a convent, and thus kept free from attachments. All the Andorrans are landed proprietors, some owning more, others less land, but there are no gentry.’ ‘The wealth of an Andorran,’ says Mr. Murray, ‘consists in sheep, cattle, or a share in a forge.
