[[cont]]A lodging is no home. It is a sort of prison. Shut up in one’s two rooms, condemned to eat day after day the same tasteless, ill-dressed, dried-up food—<i>toujours perdrix</i> might be bad enough, but <i>toujours</i> mutton-chops—or roast meat on Saturday, cold on Sunday, ditto on Monday, till in utter despair on Tuesday you give it to your dog! And as for boarding-houses, no one who has not lived in them, can imagine the petty spite, the jealousies, the unprovoked calumnies spread about all who are in any way superior to the others. One runs from them to a lodging to escape ill-nature, after trying in vain to oblige everybody in the house; and the want of proper food and consequent illness, drives one back to those refuges for the destitute, till even the inconveniences and discomforts of a foreign tour undertaken on slender means, with its changes of scene, its varied and majestic scenery, and its beautiful flowers, seems a desirable alternative. But, ah! one envies the poorest cottager in Yorkshire who has her own home and garden, her bed of flowers before the door, her garth, and her wee haystack, her pig, her chickens, and her cow!
