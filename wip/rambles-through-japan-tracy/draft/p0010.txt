Written by many instalments during each day, wherever a halt was made for rest—at wayside tea-houses, on the steps of a temple, or at the foot of a shrine, in the midst, oftentimes, of a multitude of eager and curious faces—such a record of experience is rather a series of mental photographs of things as they were seen, than the more carefully toned picture which unaided memory would probably have painted at a later date.

It has the disadvantage not only of unavoidable egotism in expression, but also of occasional repetitions; for more than once the same peculiar incident or curious custom was seen and noted down. Some historical references here and there have been slightly amplified from the original memoranda; the last two or three chapters are condensed from the diary; and some of the latest facts in regard to its public affairs have been added, as of value to every one interested in the gradual development of the country and its progressive transformation.
