These acts were duly reported; but, owing to the indiscreet anxiety of a friend to discover the action of the disease, the fact of the disease at Dunvegan became public, there was an outcry from Europe, the Company was blamed, and the unfortunate Mr. Round was made the scapegoat.

Now it is hard for me to believe that if the control of the Company had been in the hands of any one who had personal knowledge of Mr. Round’s character and service, so cruel a punishment would have been inflicted for so small a fault—if fault indeed it was; for the inquiry which was promised into the nature of the disease has not, so far as I am aware, ever been made. And that is why I alluded just now to the five years’ Commissionership: the chief of the Company has no knowledge, can have no knowledge, of his officers, and in a moment may part with one of the best the Company has ever had, inflicting a double wrong—one on the organisation that has been so well, so loyally served, another on the man who has given the prime of his life to its service.
