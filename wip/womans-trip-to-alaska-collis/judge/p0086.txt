I have, therefore, made it a rule of my travels that my palate and my gastronomy should be well cared for, if possible, in order that I may keep on such good terms with myself as to receive the various impressions of my journey in an amiable and contented mood. So much for the philosophy of my dinner at the “Poodle Dog,” and now for the realistic part of it. The <i>maître d’hôtel</i> served it <i>in propria persona</i>, and the meal was opened with the shrimps as an incentive to appetite, I presume; if so, they well performed their mission, for we were all seized with an appetite for—more shrimps; following these came Olympia oysters, in the shell, not one of which was larger than a nickel, with only just a delicate suggestion of the coppery flavor which, to my uncultivated taste, spoils the English “native”; and now, while we were busy with a broiled spring chicken,—and such a chicken, so white and so tender,—our good host informed us that he had forgotten when we inquired for game that he had a squab pheasant in the refrigerator, and he would cook it at once, if we desired. “Roast it before the fire, and serve with bread sauce,” was the
