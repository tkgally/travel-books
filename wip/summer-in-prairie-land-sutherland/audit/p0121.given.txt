Coming a second time to the river’s bank, we found a bridge, composed of poles and brush, and by careful management got the teams safely over, though how the horses managed it without breaking their legs I shall never be able to explain. The route for a few miles farther lay through a succession of swamps, where the travelling was very laborious. We passed many Indians on their way to Edmonton. A few had horses; but the greater part were travelling on foot, while a few had dogs laden with blankets and camp-kettles, trudging patiently behind their masters. Farther or we passed several cultivated fields, belonging to Indians, showing that some of them are beginning to settle down as tillers of the soil. Soon our eyes were gladdened by a sight of the telegraph line which follows the proposed route of the Canada Pacific Railway; and at 4 p.m. we found ourfelves on the bank of the North Saskatchewan, with Fort Edmonton in full view on the opposite side. One the Company’s inland boats was obtained; two wagons, loads and all, were put on board; and, propelled by strong arms, the boat was soon at the landing-place. Our horses had to swim the river, a feat which they successfully accomplished, although the distance was considerable and the current pretty strong. Half an hour later we were kindly received at the hospitable home of R. Hardisty, Esq., the gentlemanly Chief Factor of the Hudson’s Bay Company in the Upper Saskatchewan District.
