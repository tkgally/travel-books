My eyes fell next on the farmhouse itself. It was a long, rambling, one-storied farmhouse, and from one window the light of a tallow candle shone out into the shadows. There were no blinds to the window, and I could see the interior of the room more distinctly than if I had been indoors myself. It was apparently the living-room. A gleam of burnished copper utensils upon a high-backed dresser gave it a homely appearance, and there was a square wooden trestle-table in the centre of the room. Sitting beside it, bending over an open book, was an old woman, made beautiful by the countless wrinkles that seamed her fine face. It was a face that Rembrandt would have painted—a picture of age, still majestic in its decay. Close to that old woman stood a young girl in the first glory of her youth, straight and upright as a young birch tree. The contrast was violent—the splendid vigour of the young girl made age so helpless. Age is a foe that comes to all. Even that young girl in all her rude health had already passed the border-line, and day by day, hour by hour, minute by minute, henceforward that grim shadow would steal an infinitesimal morsel of her bloom.
