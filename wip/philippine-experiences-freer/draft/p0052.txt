[[cont]] of these women wore rich costumes—long-trained skirts of brocaded silk in bright colors and loose upper garments of fine <i>piña</i> handsomely embroidered; and some were guilty of encasing their feet, accustomed to the comfort of <i>chinelas,</i> in high-heeled and narrow-toed slippers. An orchestra of fifteen instruments began playing as the waiters filled our glasses with wine. Then followed course after course of food prepared in Spanish style—fish, venison in several different modes and courses, chicken, goat’s flesh, patties and other dishes which have no English names. Few vegetables were served. The dessert consisted of a rich pudding made of native chocolate, sugar, <i>carabao’s</i> milk, eggs and rice flour; luscious bananas, and coffee of native growth. All of the food was well prepared, very palatable and nicely served. For the dancing, the orchestra and the town brass band alternated in playing; beer and cigars were served, Philippine etiquette permitting smoking at all such functions. The party did not break up until two o’clock in the morning, when our wagon rattled its way homeward to Solano by moonlight, while the occupants sang “I Was Seeing Nellie Home” and other old songs.
