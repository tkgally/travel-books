We reached the theatre at two o’clock, and after picking our way through the heaps of slippers and clogs, which had been left at the entrance by the audience, were shown in to the box which we had reserved. The house was crammed, and the audience seemed to be composed about equally of members of both the sexes. The men in Japan as a rule vote the theatre slow, and whilst they send the ladies to the play, go themselves to the tea-houses; but Danjuro’s talent appeals to all alike.

The curtain had just been drawn aside and a new act had begun. Danjuro, in the character of the proud usurper, was seated on his throne surrounded by courtiers, who were loading him with fulsome flattery, when Kiriguro, another famous comedian, approached in disguise as a messenger from the King’s rival. This actor wore a pair of inordinately long cotton trousers, and the surplus material trailed behind him in a very comical manner, as he strutted about the stage.

A little later Danjuro appeared as the humble young maiden of sixteen, deeply in love with a royal prince. She was betrayed into avowing this passion to the old court dames, who flaunted and chaffed her, until she became frantic with rage at their jeers and ill-treatment.
