The umbrage, if I may so call it, seemed to welcome our arrival; it actually made apparent reverence to us, or, at least, we might imagine it: for really, to see all Nature bowing down, as we did, over the pathway of unknown and unheard-of visitors, was a welcome of Nature to Christianity,—so I, poetically, thought it to be.

The underground was of dwarf oak, ferns, roses, myrtaceæ, and many leguminosæ, which I cannot here describe. Then the orchis, of all plants the dearest, several “mimosæ,” calytris (on the sand), “Dodonæas,” “Hakeas,” and a creeper almost like the sensitive plant (<i>M. sensitiva</i>).

Our first halt was at a village, “quod versu dicere non est,” about twenty miles from Hakodate in a straight line. We were most kindly received everywhere, and here we entered the first “Aino” village.
