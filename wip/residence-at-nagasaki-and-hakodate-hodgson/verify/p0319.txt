They are mighty men, backed by proud and insolent adventurers for attendants, who clear the road without any regard for person or nation, and compel the rabble to do obeisance to the grandeur of their chiefs. At Yukohama, indeed, I remember walking with the French Minister, when two Secretaries of State for Foreign Affairs were paying a visit to the Governor, and no person, except M. de Bellecourt, who was known to their Excellencies, and who politely saluted him as he passed them, was allowed to break the line. Everywhere I there observed the submissive Japanese on their knees, or in the act of making their obeisance; but it was done as a matter of course, had been the habit for centuries, and was perhaps considered by them to be no greater humiliation than our capping or taking off of hats. Indeed, it is not long since our knees were wont to bend to princes, while, in the days of good Queen Bess, her passage through the capital was almost as submissive a spectacle.

The Chinese servants, proud of their minority, and relying on the protection of the British flag, would stand up boldly during the passage of the ministers, and no doubt the sight was far from pleasing to men who have been so long accustomed to regard these Celestials with any other feeling than that of inferiority.
