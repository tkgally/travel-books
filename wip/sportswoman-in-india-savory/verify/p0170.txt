The reason it was hard work was on account of the rarefied atmosphere; we were climbing between fourteen and fifteen thousand feet above sea level, and until you have grown used to this altitude breathing is laborious. The Jemadar and Māmdīn gave us both a hand now and then. After a spurt of this sort—panting—we were allowed a rest, till Māmdīn came up and said sternly, “<i>Chel!—chel!</i> Mees Sahib” (“get up!—get up!”). We meekly rose to renewed efforts. Whenever we asked how much farther, the answer was invariably, “<i>Oōper.</i>”

At one time we had to ford a loud-tongued, rollicking stream of ice-cold water, in which immense pine-logs had gone aground, not to be floated off till the next <i>spate</i>. When one saw the white planks also far below in the distance, strewing the bed of a rather dried-up stream, they reminded us of a train of spilt lucifer matches.

There was a blaze of sunshine everywhere, a universal glitter which I never saw till I came to India; this, together with the elasticity of the air, banished every feeling of weariness and gave one spirit for anything. The mountains rose like castellated and embattled walls round us, skirted and crowned with dark pines, which occasionally parted to show some snow-slashed peak beyond, rising into the intense, unclouded blue sky.
