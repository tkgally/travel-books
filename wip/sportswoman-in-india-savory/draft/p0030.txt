[[cont]]crammed with “specials.” We stationed ourselves on a small mound, under some shady trees; the eclipse began about midday, when the moon could just be seen overlapping the edge of the sun. Through smoked glasses, telescopes, and glasses of every kind, the great concourse of people gazed at it, and the excitement grew intense as the moon drew farther and farther across the sun. It became perceptibly cooler. The thousands of spectators, watches in hand, were almost breathless as 1.43 p.m. approached—the time of totality. The sky began to turn blue-green like twilight, the stretches of burnt yellow plain around us assumed a brown hue, which spread over the horizon and the sky immediately above it. Every man’s face turned a horrid, sickly yellow in the weird light. Dimmer and yet more dim—a hush was over the murmuring crowd; 1.43, and the moon glided entirely across the sun, showing us nothing but a large black body hanging in the sky. Venus glimmered through the green and yellow haze, and another star or two shone, the grass looked more purple than before, and the colour of the whole dark world was unique. . . . Two minutes passed, and then suddenly a bright light flashed from the edge of the moon. The Sun!—The Sun! The tension was over, a wild cheer broke simultaneously from the whole throng as the brilliant edge of the lord of creation slid from behind its temporary screen, and once more lit the earth.
