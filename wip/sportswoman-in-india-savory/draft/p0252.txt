Next morning we had a heavy business getting all our baggage-ponies safely over the ridge above the tarn; but it was done at last, and the rest of the march, downhill all the way to Lidderwat, was simple enough—some of the finest country too, in Kashmir. Wandering up on these heights possesses a fascination not to be met with in marching through the valleys.

By the boulders of the glacier torrents we came across numerous <i>burra chuars</i> (big rats—as the natives call marmots). They live high up in the snowy regions of the mountains, generally preferring exposed cliffs or stony expanses, whence they may have a clear view of approaching danger.

The first thing we heard was a piercing whistle, shrill and uncanny, and looking about, at last discovered a marmot seated on the top of a rock over his own burrow. He was about three feet long, and had reddish brown fur with a black stripe down his back; he sat up on his hind-legs at ’tention, acting sentinel, while his relatives were basking in the sun or else running actively about in search of food. His shrill and impertinent little whistle told us we had been seen, and directly we tried to get closer he whisked into his burrow.
