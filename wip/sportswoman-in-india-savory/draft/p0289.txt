Lalla was by this time wrought up into the highest pitch of excitement; indeed, it was quite impossible to be an inert spectator, and I prayed that something, at least, might appear. But no; the “watched pot” never boiled! On came the war-cries, nearer and nearer; still there was nothing.

At last the straggling line of coolies began to appear; alas! there was no <i>hārpāt</i> in that beat. It was peculiarly irritating, and exactly like natives, that the nearer they got to us, and even when they were within a few yards, the louder they yelled and shouted; we were obliged to pelt them with a pebble or two to induce them to cease their maddening noise.

One thing was clear at least, that they beat extremely badly, ten or twelve of them going together in a clump, and never properly beating out a nullah at all. The headman was harangued and rated on this point; and then we walked off to have another <i>honk.</i>

Again we found ourselves on high ground, and after half an hour’s wait the beaters began. We were hidden, waiting in perfect silence for twenty minutes, perhaps, listening to the tom-toms and the shouts rising and falling, when all in a moment they changed into a piercing clamour, and a yell arose from a hundred and fifty throats. “<i>Hārpāt! hārpāt!</i>” we could almost distinguish.
