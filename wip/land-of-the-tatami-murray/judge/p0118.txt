The view from the fifth storey was wonderful—all Japan seemed to pass before my eyes in its beauty and enchantment. I took twelve snapshots of the castle and its environs, which I intend to nurse into everlasting memory. The Japanese people look upon this ancient stronghold with awe and veneration; it is sacred to them as a symbol of those times when their mighty manhood was cradled in endurance and stoic contempt of death and danger.

Of Russian prisoners of war there are at present in Himeji about nine hundred. These are mostly sailors and a great many of them hail from the <i>Rurik.</i> They are treated—how shall I term it? well; too well, indeed. A large party of them were employed in playing a kind of Russian base-ball, which they seemingly enjoyed very much, without giving much thought to their comrades in present battle, except inasmuch as considering themselves d——lucky to be—where they were. Well fed, well housed, compulsory cleanliness gently enforced (alas! their only punishment), in communication with their people at home, indeed fate had been kind to them. To me, who immediately after visited the wounded Japanese, they looked like barbarians and oh! how very much inferior to their captors! Strict instructions have been issued by the War Office in Tokio to allow nobody to converse with these prisoners; I did not demur; no doubt very good reasons existed for causing these orders. Spies sneak about in all disguises, and lies would willingly be told against their generous jailors, by some of the loyal members of the fourth estate, who dearly love Russians (<i>roubles</i>) and who secretly gnash their teeth at each successive Japanese victory. I was informed that an order from Tokio to converse with these prisoners of war could be procured for me. if I so desired, but I did not insist. <i>Vide et crede!</i>
