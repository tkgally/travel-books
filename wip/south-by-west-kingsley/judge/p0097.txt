After we got back, Miss J. and I made our beds, put the room straight, and sat reading and writing all the morning, till it was time for dinner, when we made another rush for the restaurant.

The snow was drifting tremendously, the strong wind lifting the dry powdery particles off the ground, and blowing it across the plain in clouds of white dust. The thermometer outside our house registered 13° above zero,—19° of frost. The train, we thought, would of course be stopped by drifts on the Divide: but it was only one hour late; and, in the middle of dinner, in it steamed. It was really a fine sight. The little ‘Cortez’ had been through the snow-drifts, up to the top of the lamp in front of the chimney. The wheels, and every ledge and corner, were a mass of snow, and the icicles hung in a crystal fringe all along the boiler.

W., the engineer, came in to dinner, looking, as they said, “pretty wild, as if he had had a struggle for it,” and said he thought they would not get back to Denver before morning, as the wind would be against them. So Miss J. decided to stay with me another day: while her brother and another official determined to risk it, and go back to Denver by the afternoon train.
