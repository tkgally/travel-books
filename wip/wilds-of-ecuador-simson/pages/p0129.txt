We were therefore compelled to have recourse to our usual dishes: fowl, yuca, and sweet potatoes, with sago and eggs. The Indians themselves are not averse to cooked flesh which even passes the state designated by civilised communities as “high.” We found here a new kind of potato, a long thick tuber of the finest texture and flavour ever bestowed upon a root. It is called “<i>mándi,</i>” and is infinitely superior to the slimy potato of Canelos. Our men say that the <i>mándi</i> is also known on the Pintuc and at Baños, though we had not seen it at either of those places.

One of the Indians who visited us was the handsomest specimen of his race that we had so far seen anywhere; and one could hardly imagine his tall, massive structure, and frank, benevolent countenance to be surpassed in any country. I made him a present of a briarwood pipe, with which he was evidently very much pleased.

The next day commenced with an involuntary bath, with clothes on, as we had to cross the Villano at the start. Leaving the left bank of this river, we shortly came to a smaller torrent, the Chitata, which flows into it, and which was followed up a long way, partly in the water and partly over the stones and boulders, occasionally also cutting off the sharp curves in its course by passing through the bordering forest.
