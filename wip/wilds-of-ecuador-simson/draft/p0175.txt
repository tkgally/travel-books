The cloth being worth 1½ reales, or about 6<i>d.</i> a yard, the total payment comes to less than 18<i>s.</i> Out of the thirty yards of cloth, moreover, the recipient generally makes 2½ yards into a <i>poncho</i>, 1½ into a pair of drawers, 6 into a “<i>toldo,</i>” to protect him from the voracious mosquitoes of the Marañon. The remainder in most cases probably goes to wife and children, and thus the earnings are employed before the journey commences, and the cloth is often worn out before the voyage is completed.

At starting and arriving, festivities of the usual class, in which <i>chicha</i> drinking constitutes the main feature, are carried on, and the travellers shove off their canoes in setting out, and hail their friends, as they heave in sight on returning, with the sound of the cattle horns procured from the Marañon, and upon which they blow. Were the journey commenced or completed without tootooing on the “<i>bobona,</i>” the travellers would be considered to cut but a sorry figure, and, to say the least, would pass among their friends as unsophisticated greenhorns, destitute of the noblest attributes of a great traveller! Most of them would rather suffer anything than return to their native village unannounced by the <i>bobona.</i>
