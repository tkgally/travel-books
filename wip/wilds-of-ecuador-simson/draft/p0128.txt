At the first day’s halt the camp was pitched between two little streams which have cut their way deep into the soil, and are called the “Tsatsapi Chiquito” and “Tsatsapi grande,” about an hour’s distance from each other.

The following morning I again heard the “<i>flautero</i>” performing his attractive song. All day the road lay in a northerly direction, along the tops of <i>cuchillas</i> and up and down hill. About half-an-hour’s walk from the camping ground brought us to “Tsatsapi Grande,” and two hours more of very fast going to the river Villano, a tributary of the Curarai, so that we had at length reached Napo waters, having as far as Canelos been journeying down the Pastassa valley.

The Villano was descended by walking in its bed and crossing it frequently by wading, till we came to several tambos, or small huts, situated along its banks and forming the “country residences” of some of the Canelos Indians. All these Indians have similar tambos, to which they can retire away from life in the village, and where probably game is more abundant and less hunted after.
