One dries his corn in the capacious oven, or

<blockquote>
“Weaves the pliant basket of bramble twigs,”
</blockquote>

or slowly rears his wattle fence of the yielding cane. One flits from hearth to hearth with a potsherd of live coals.

Others bear on their heads great bundles of flax from the fields; while others again bruise, hackle, spin, and wind it ready for the loom. Few are idle. Their patient toil and their simple lives are full of lessons for us. They show us how circumscribed is the limit of the actual necessities of life, and our own extravagance and wastefulness as individuals and as a nation. Many of them never look over the walls of the crater in which they were born. They work from sunrise to sunset for about a shilling a day. Their food is corn-bread and a drink of spring-water, with now and then a few bitter beans and a bit of dry fish as luxuries. They have no barns nor storehouses; for there is no grass to cut, the corn is housed with the family, and the hens and pigeons roost among the thatch. Most of them own neither field nor cart, nor ox nor horse, nor donkey nor cow nor goat. They have neither tea nor coffee, and seldom taste a drop of milk.
