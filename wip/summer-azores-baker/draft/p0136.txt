Between these ridges lie richly cultivated valleys, sloping up from the sea to the base of the central range; and the shore at the foot of these valleys is indented, forming little hemispherical bays between the headlands. Along these bays lie the villages of Madeira.

The land seems to rise in very narrow, natural terraces from the sea, back upon the mountain-slopes, and in the intervening valleys. These terraces are of the most vivid green, being devoted to the culture of the sugar-cane. Sugar-mills are planted here and there along the shore. For twenty miles, we steam along quite near the shore. Soon the terraces disappear; and instead of sloping sierras, the mountain-spurs terminate abruptly in magnificent precipices, with sheer faces from one to two thousand feet in height. These cliffs are of lava, of the general color of our old red sandstone, with broad seams of leady black, of burnt sienna, and of pure vermilion, crossing their faces in irregular lines. Their beauty of color, grandeur of form, and sublimity of height are indescribable.
