[[cont]]cart drawn by an ox and a cow yoked together. It is made from one piece of wood, with a wicker body. Its solid wheels and heavy axle slowly revolve together with a terrific creak, dear to the heart of the Fayalese peasant. There was formerly a law that the axles must be soaped before entering the city. A revolution occurring as to the basis of taxation,[[fnref 1]] some Azorean Danton demanded redress of grievances. “Down with the income tax,” he cried: “give us tithes!” “Tithes,” echoed the mob, “and liberty to squeak our carts through the streets!” The wicker cart of the Azores is like the Roman <i>plaustrum</i> with its <i>tympana</i> or solid orbed wheels, to which Virgil often alludes, and to which he constantly applies the adjectives <i>gementia</i> and <i>stridentia.</i>

Yonder is a group of women at a well. Their tall wooden buckets, shaped like old-fashioned wooden churns and holding six or seven gallons, stand on the stone curb.
