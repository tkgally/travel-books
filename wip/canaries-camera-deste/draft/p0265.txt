At first they could only gaze at us in silence, but presently an intelligent-looking man assumed the <i>rôle</i> of spokesman and turning to the others asked them if they had ever heard of a <i>traje de pais</i> or <i>traje de fiesta</i> such as the señoras were inquiring about.

One and all shook their heads, passing the phrases we had used from mouth to mouth with a solemnity worthy of the chorus of a comic opera.

The oldest inhabitant, who had tottered up on crutches, was now appealed to, and pointing with a quavering hand to the English guidebook I was holding, intimated that he would first like to see what was written there on the subject; poring over the page—which was less than Greek to him, since he assuredly had never learned to read—he returned us the volume, and nodding to the spokesman of the party assured him that what the señoras said about an old costume was quite true, for it was set forth in print.

Spurred to fresh efforts our friend despatched a girl to a neighbouring house whence she presently issued, wearing a bright pink silk blouse, a blue skirt, a necklace of glass beads, and a fly-away hat decked out with ostrich feathers. “Señora!” said the man triumphantly—leading the girl, whose cheeks were white with pearl-powder, in front of the camera—“behold the fête-day costume, <i>traje de fiesta!</i>”

We were reluctant to dash his pleasure, but we were forced to tell him that that was not what we wanted.
