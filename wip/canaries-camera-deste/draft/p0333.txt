A few years ago sugar-cane was largely grown around Guia, but now the mills stand idle. Cochineal still fetches one and tenpence a pound, and a few acres of land are devoted to it; but the banana has practically ousted all rivals in the district, and the whole population seems to be employed in attending upon it.

We looked into a shed from whence there came a sound of lusty hammering, and saw hundreds of banana bunches being packed for export. A woman was standing on either side of a stock of newspapers spread open upon the floor, and as each bunch was laid down it was enfolded—before being put into its crate—first in a wrapping of cotton wool and then in a new and uncut sheet of the <i>Lancashire Daily Post</i>, hundreds of copies of which seemed to have found no better use than to come out to Guia as wrapping material. Even now when in the streets of London I notice a van piled high with crates and see the straw or the tattered brown banana leaves sticking out through the slats it brings back the memory of that packing party in the shed, and of the mule-teams—hot, thirsty, strenuous—shuffling along in clouds of white dust on their six hours' journey to Las Palmas.

While we were at Guia we visited an old Canario cave at Galdar, a couple of miles away; turning aside out of one of the streets of the little town, a short but odoriferous walk through some onion fields brought us to the cave, which is below the level of the ground and was discovered quite accidentally some twenty-five years ago.
