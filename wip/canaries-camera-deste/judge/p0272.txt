# Chapter XVII

THE following morning we rose early, with a view to visiting the <i>Gran Caldera;</i> by the local time it was five o’clock, and on going to our windows what was our disappointment to see the sky heavily overcast and the crests of the mountains already enveloped in clouds. Tourists however cannot choose their weather, and we set out, consoling ourselves with the reflection that there was at least as good a chance of the day improving as of its growing worse.

The next three hours’ riding was the roughest we had as yet encountered—which is saying a good deal. On topping the brow of the nearest hill one finds oneself looking down into the <i>Barranco de las Angustias</i>—a ravine so gigantic that it might almost be termed a valley, were it not for the precipitous nature of its sides. Opposite us, on the far side of the gulf, rises a sheer wall of black lava seventeen hundred feet in height—the barrier of the Timé (pronounced Tee-may), and far below, flowing down the bed of the <i>barranco</i> and dwarfed by distance to a mere thread, is the stream that issues from the great basin of the <i>Caldera</i>—the only stream of even respectable size that we ever saw in the Canaries.
