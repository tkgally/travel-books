The Foo-yen, or Governor of Canton, was likewise still solicitous to discover the private objects of the Embassy; and aware that some of them might relate to persons from whom the commissioners would wish them to be concealed at this early period, he gave assurances to the commissioners that, “if they should disclose them to him, he would confine the matter within his own breast, and that of the Emperor.” The commissioners very properly replied that “they were ignorant of any views beside those which obviously occurred of paying a just compliment to his Imperial Majesty, and of cultivating his friendship; but that if there were any other, they were undoubtedly confided to the Embassador alone.”

It was perhaps in the hope of penetrating into those supposed intentions, and, if any such there were, of obstructing the progress of the Embassy, that the commissioners were repeatedly urged to write to the Embassador to stop at Canton, where all foreign vessels came, instead of proceeding to Tien-sing.
