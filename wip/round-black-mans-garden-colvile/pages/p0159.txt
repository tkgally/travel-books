As there did not seem much to make it worth while walking any longer in the hot sun, we retraced our steps and returned on board. This island is pretty, but without any particular interest, and is said to be a hotbed of fever; even at the time of our visit—the end of the dry season—it was extraordinarily green, and one could hardly imagine to what further degree of rankness the vegetation would reach by the end of the rains.

The next night was spent at anchor, a great relief after the last restless one at sea! Early on the following morning, however, we started again, and twenty-four hours’ steaming brought us to Tamatave, November the 8th, 6.30 <sc>a.m.</sc> Just before entering the harbour we passed “L’Île des Prunes,” whose green foliage offered a striking contrast to the withered tree-trunks, evidences of the force of the hurricane which had swept over it in 1885, blowing off the roofs of houses, uprooting and killing the trees, and driving on shore the ships at anchor in the harbour. I was told that the British consul, Captain Haggard, was at the Mauritius during this storm, and on his return found that the roof of his house had been lifted off like the lid of a box and deposited half a mile away.
