The word written on one side of the blade he said was ‘<i>Noser,</i>’ which he translated to mean ‘May God give me courage!’ That on the other side he said was ‘<i>Tablok.</i>’ The meaning of this word he did not know. He said that it was probably the name of the maker of the weapon.

Aïssa then began to question him, on my prompting, as to the Saharan routes and wells.

He illustrated his remarks by rough sketch maps drawn with his forefinger in the dust of the floor. He seemed to know the exact distance in days’ journey from each well and oasis to the others, and had moreover apparently an accurate knowledge of the quality and quantity of water to be found in each well. Such and such wells, he explained, now contained a certain amount of water, but in a month or two, as the weather grew hotter, they would dry up.

His knowledge of the desert appeared, too, to be up to date. At Bir el Gharama, for instance, which he described as a pool in the desert, he said that the water, though usually good, was at that time bad, as a dead camel had been floating in it all the winter. On the subject of Saharan geography he was clearly an expert.
