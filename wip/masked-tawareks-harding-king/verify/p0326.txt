This <i>amghar</i> is, in theory, elected, and, if necessary, dismissed by popular vote, but practically the chieftaincy, and, in fact, nearly all property among the Tawareks, descends, in accordance with the rules of a curious law of heredity, almost invariably from one member of the ruling family to another.

The Tawareks trace their descent through the female line. On the death therefore of a chief the office goes, with the approval of the people, to the eldest son of his eldest sister. In no case does it go to any of his sons.

Mohammedanism is nominally the religion of the Tawareks. But their faith in the <i>jinns</i> and other spirits of the Moslem world is so strong as almost to eclipse the idea of the supreme Allah—they might almost be called devil-worshippers.

The Arabs say that the Tawareks have no religion at all. They profess, however, to adhere to the Malekite section of the Sunnis. Senoussism, as has already been stated, claims a very large number of adherents among them. Their prayers, when they say them, are said in Arabic. The only time we saw a Tawarek praying, Aïssa, who, though very slack in his own religious observances, was very much down in any slackness in others, declared in great disgust that he was going through the prostrations and flexions which play such an important part in the Moslem ritual all wrong, and suggested to me that he should turn <i>marabout</i> for the occasion and give that ungodly Tawarek a lesson in saying his prayers
