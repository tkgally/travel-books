But probably they had not sailed a great-circle course from Vancouver, as our previous expedition did. Now, after traversing its enchanting southern water spaces, with day after day of shining sea, and trade-winds, with no necessity for racks on the table or “fences” at night—these things quite obliterated all memory of that other unfriendly northern Pacific which in 1887 had treated the old Abyssinia so unceremoniously. Except for a day or two, this voyage had been a tropical harmony in blue and gold.

And after this one tempestuous night, the morning dawned fair and lovely, but greeted no longer by a sapphire sea to reflect the brilliant sky. The Coronet was unmistakably in the <i>Kurosiwa</i>, the “black current” of Japan; the water was dark green, and full of drifting sea-weed.

Before sunset of that bright Sunday, the twenty-first of June, two or three islands appeared,—Mikura, Miaki, and Vries. Then the incomparable cone of Fuji lifted itself against the sky—that well remembered landmark which was our last sight of the beautiful land nine years before, and without which Japan could not be Japan.
