Their Imperial Majesties, the Emperor and Empress, came at once and nobly to the rescue, the Mitsui family contributed scarcely less, and the Tokyo journals opened subscriptions for relief of starving survivors, the “Jiji” collecting in a few days over ten thousand yen, and the “Nichi-Nichi” more than eight thousand, while the Iwate branch of the Japan Red Cross Society established temporary hospitals among the suffering people. The number of victims was at first greatly underrated.

All the habits, even the methods of thought, return in plunging once more into a land as distinctly foreign as Japan, and even in Yokohama, where strangers from other countries most abound, the native atmosphere is hardly adulterated enough to change the general effect.
