After a while a certain commotion up stream indicated we were near the famous fishing. Six brilliant lights seemed drifting downward, and in a moment they surrounded us. Six boats had each an iron cage swung forward over the water, full of brightly burning wood which threw a wide glare. In each bow stood a man holding twelve cords attached to as many cormorants, large, black water birds, struggling and screaming and diving in every direction. Not to tangle all those lines required the skill of a circus driver, as each bird went its own way in search of the fish it instantly swallowed.

But the unusual part of this method of fishing is that a heavy iron ring at the base of the cormorant's neck is so tight as to allow only the smallest fish to pass through. All others lodge in the throat, and when that is full the bird is hauled back into the boat, and made to disgorge what it has just been at such trouble to obtain. That a bird should thereafter immediately desire to go fishing again seems odd, but its ardor is unabated, and it rushes once more into the fray with ever new enthusiasm. Three thousand of the <i>ai</i>, a sort of trout, is not a large evening's catch for a single boat.
