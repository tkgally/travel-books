Except that they have graveyards, and do not bury their dead promiscuously all over the country, the Japanese seem to treat their deceased relations in much the same manner as the Chinese, for though one sect “cremate” and the other do not, all unite in the respect paid to their ancestors.

As in China, there are certain seasons of the year more especially set apart for feasts in honour of the dead. On these occasions the spirits of the departed are supposed to be present, and when all the rites are concluded, they are sent off again to the place whence they came in little boats neatly modelled in straw, in which are put rice, water, and a small lamp to light them on their way. Numbers of these little boats may be seen on the “Inland Sea” at some periods of the year.

Economy of space does not prevail in any other respect, saving that of their places of interment, for the streets are all wide, even those in the native city, and such is the march of intellect, that they are lighted with gas, and have pillar letter-posts in all directions.
