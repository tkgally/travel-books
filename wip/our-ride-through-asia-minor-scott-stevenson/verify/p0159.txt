Its history is very interesting, both from a classical and biblical point of view. It was the same centre of commerce in the eastern basin of the Mediterranean as Marseilles was in the western. It possessed one of the three great universities of the world; Strabo ranks it before Athens or Alexandria. The imperial family of Rome used to select tutors for their sons from here. Another peculiarity was the fact, that whilst all the youth of the then civilised world, used to resort to Athens and Alexandria, and these cities swarmed with foreigners, few strangers came to Tarsus; thus the natives laid up a store of knowledge at home, distributing it later in their travels, and more especially in Rome, where they became the chief instructors of the rising generation.

This fact perhaps accounts for St. Paul's reputation as a scholar. But the whole of Cilicia was not like Tarsus. The inhabitants in the surrounding plain and mountains were still “barbarians,” and the city with its refinement stood alone in its beautiful plains in the midst of a rude population, who used a different language, and possessed no literature of its own.
