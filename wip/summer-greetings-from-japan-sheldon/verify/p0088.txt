There are six or eight men to a cell, with electric light burning in the cell all night. We went through all the shops. There are some thirty different trades or kinds of work carried on. In each shop as we entered there was a sudden call, every man took position, and then every head bowed solemnly in unison, and at once they all resumed work. As the average sentence is only two years, I asked the warden if any came back again. “Yes,” he said, “most of them,” especially for stealing. How many times? “Twenty,” he answered. And so these people have not solved the prison problem, and they know it. They also have a chapel, where a Buddhist priest preaches every Sunday. The warden spoke German, but no English, so that we got along in that language. Of course the <i>grounds</i> were like a beautiful garden of trees and shrubs, as everywhere else in Japan. In the kitchen there was <i>rice</i>, and lots ot it. Am sorry these people have nothing to teach us on the prison system. My guide, Mr. Tomeoka, was very well informed. He has charge of a <i>Home Reformatory</i> for boys. <i>That</i> is up-to-date in idea. He knew all about Elmira and Mr. Brockway, and the system of the “indeterminate sentence.” They <i>will</i> yet have it here, I am sure, for they know how to be up-to-date, even if they are not inventive. On leaving there, I came back for an hour's talk again with Count Okuma. This time I sounded <i>him</i> on religion, but I could not get much out of him on that subject. Well, good-night! I must get up at midnight for a visit to the police-station.
