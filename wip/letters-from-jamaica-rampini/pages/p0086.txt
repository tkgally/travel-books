[[cont]]“out-children,” or “love-children,” as they are called,—generally follow the mother. It is rare that a father thinks himself bound even to provide food for such children. He bears them no affection: he does not recognise any claim they may have upon him. If they lose themselves, and die in the woods, he does not deem himself bound to go and bury them. It is the woman’s look-out, not his. Why should he trouble himself with what does not concern him?

It must be kept in view that a negro’s thoughts, and cares, and feelings are bounded by this world alone. “It is almost impossible,” said a worthy clergyman to me, “to make him believe in a place of eternal punishment.” Death to the negro has no terrors. He dies because his time has come. But he dies, like a dog, without a regret and without a pang,—confident that, if there is a heaven, he will find admission there; and that, if there is not, he has finished his course, and drunk all that he will ever be allowed to drink of the pleasures and pains of life.

Fatalism is in itself a superstition, and the negro is superstitious in the extreme. A belief in “duppies” (ghosts) is universal. The spirits of the bad “walk,” as the negroes call it, and after death revisit the spots they have known on earth. They have their favourite haunts too.
