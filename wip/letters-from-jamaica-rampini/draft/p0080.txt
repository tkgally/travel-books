Then stray specimens of the golden fern appeared, and we saw several varieties of the superb vine or parasitic ferns creeping over bush and shrub, and hanging in long elegant garlands to the ground. At times a fresh cold breeze came sweeping over the path, and now and then came the “cling-cling” of a black-bird, or the “sweet, sweet, sweet to-too” of the fly-catcher (<i>vires Noveboracensis</i>).

Very quaint and very amusing are some of the negro’s interpretations of the songs of the birds—

<verse>
“liquidas avium voces”—
</verse>

and the sounds of external nature.

The jabbering crow (<i>Corvus Jamaicensis</i>), a wise and learned bird, in harsh guttural tones, gabbles forth

<verse>
“Walk fast crab, do buckra work,
Cutacoo[[fnref 1]] better than wallet.”
</verse>

“Tom Paine, Tom Paine!” whistles out the black and golden banana bird (<i>Icterus leucopteryx</i>); “Going awa-a-y!” cries the Savanna black-bird (<i>Crotophaga ani</i>); “Gi’ me a bit,” says the night or mosquito hawk (<i>Chordeiles Virginianus</i>); and the white-wing dove (<i>Turtur leucopteryx</i>), in protracted and moaning tones, announces that “Since poor Gilpin die, cow-head spoil!” Vain of her plumage the female of the blue pigeon (<i>Columba rufina</i>) cries
