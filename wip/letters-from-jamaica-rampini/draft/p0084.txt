# VIII.
[[subtitle]] Blacks and Whites—A Contrast.

A <sc>man’s</sc> estimate of the negro character varies according to the length of time he spends among them. The first year, his opinion of them is a high one. He is amused by their merry faces, their broad grins, their apparent good nature, their seeming simplicity of character. He looks upon them as happy children, for whom the song, and the dance, and the church constitute the essentials of life. The second year his ideas are somewhat modified. He regards them as children still, but now as spoiled children, who give a great deal of trouble; and by the third or fourth year he has begun to talk about the “irrepressible nigger,” and to speak of them in very much the same language as the planters get the credit of doing.
