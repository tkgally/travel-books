Presently we were approached by an emissary of Ahmid es Sultaneh, who came to receive our orders as to when and where we should like to have our horses, so at least he said. As a matter of fact he came to tell us what the programme was that had been arranged for us, and we very soon discovered that this programme would have to be very strictly adhered to if we did not wish our escort to “eat the stick,” as the poetical Persians say. For in Persia the verb to eat is used very loosely and vaguely. Thus, when a man falls he is said to “eat the earth,” when he is drowned he “eats the water”; in short, it would seem that there was nothing that an able-bodied Persian could not eat.

As we sat on our veranda, gazing on the stars and into the darkness, we could hear in the distance, and proceeding from the other side of the Murghab, an uncanny sound of barking.

“Those are the jackals. If you hear them replying from the other side it means that we shall have a fine day to-morrow,” said Churchill.

But they did not reply.
