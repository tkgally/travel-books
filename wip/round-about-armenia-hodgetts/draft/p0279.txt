It was appropriately just before luncheon.

“That is to remind you fellows of us,” I said, grimly, at which the Cossack laughed very good-naturedly. Just off the Balaklava Heights I looked up and saw the Balaklava monument, and I thought of that terrible day when the British army saw from those heights the transports, laden with the supplies and the warm clothing they so much needed, dash themselves to pieces against the rocks before their very eyes.

As we steamed into Sevastopol Harbour we saw unfolded before us the imposing panorama of the Black Sea Fleet; but when we got nearer to the individual vessels I was surprised at the bad condition in which they appeared to be. Their sides were sadly in need of paint, and they looked dirty and out of repair. There was no neatness or smartness about them. The discipline seemed slack, the yards were badly trimmed, and there was a general happy-go-lucky air about everything.

There was a stoppage of several hours at Sevastopol, and so I went on shore to look at the town; but there was no time to go to see the cemetery. I am bound to say that in my walk round the town I experienced no annoyance of any kind, and I was surprised to learn later from the papers that a party of English gentlemen had not been allowed to land.
