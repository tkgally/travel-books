Feeling that he is now getting into the secrets of the language, he says, “<i>Kome</i> grows in the fields,” when he is again stopped with the information that <i>growing</i> rice is called <i>ine!</i>

He next picks up a carpenter’s rule, and is told that the foot measure is called <i>shaku</i>. He is glad to find that it is just about twelve inches in length, but is nonplussed when he learns that the tailor’s <i>shaku</i> measures fifteen inches. His perplexity increases on finding that when he sends for a <i>kin</i> (pound) of beef he gets five sixths of an avoirdupois pound; if he send for a <i>kin</i> of flour, he gets one and one third pounds; while, if he purchase a <i>kin</i> of sugar, it is within a small fraction of two pounds. In starting on a journey he is told that one <i>ri</i> is equal to two and one half English miles; but if, in passing through certain districts, he be puzzled because of the unexpectedly long distances, he may be told that there it takes three and a half miles to make a <i>ri</i>.
