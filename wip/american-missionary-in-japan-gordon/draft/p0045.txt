Our friend begins in a concrete way by inquiring the names of the most familiar things about the house, using the one sentence given him by an older missionary, <i>Kore wa nani to mōshimasu ka</i> (“What is this?”) In answer to this question he is told that the rice on the table is called <i>meshi.</i> (All vowels, it should be remarked, have the Continental pronunciation.) Rejoicing in this knowledge, he begins making sentences: “I eat <i>meshi.</i>” “The little child likes <i>meshi.</i>” “No,” says his mentor; “in speaking of a child’s rice, it is better to use the word <i>mama;</i> ‘the child likes <i>mama.</i>’” Undiscouraged, the student tries again: “Do you eat <i>meshi?</i>” when his teacher stops him, and tells him that it is polite, in speaking to another of his having or eating rice, to call it <i>gozen.</i> Having taken this in, he goes on with his sentence-building: “The merchant sells <i>gozen,</i>” when the teacher again calls a halt, and tells him
