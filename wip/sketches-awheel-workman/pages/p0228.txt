The guide-books tell what the mosque of Abd-el-Rhaman is and is not to-day, and they hint at what it was in other days. Perhaps that is all that can be expected, but the mind tries to picture it as it was when sixteen doors no longer existing opened on the <i>patio</i>, revealing endless lines of pillars within and corresponding rows of orange trees without. The many vistas of arches and marble columns have with reason been compared to a forest of palms, and with the thousand bronze and silver lamps of other days burning amid its arches, it may be imagined as having resembled an illumined pillared oasis.

The memory of the caliph who planned this wonderful <i>mesquita</i> and who daily assisted the workmen at their task is more to be cherished than that of the Christians who, when they took Cordova in 1146, used the columns of the mosque as hitching-posts for their horses. When one’s walk among the columned aisles is interrupted by the <i>coro</i>, here more odious if possible than in any other church of Spain, the fact that Charles V. regretted the sacrifice of hundreds of columns to the erection of this Christian altar in no way assuages one’s grief
