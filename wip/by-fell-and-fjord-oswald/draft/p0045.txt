About <sc>a.d.</sc> 870, Iceland was an uninhabited desert. By the middle of the next century the emigration was over, the good land occupied, and the population probably not much below its present number—about 70,000 people. This is a very small population for 38,000 square miles; but it is estimated that only a ninth part of the land has ever been permanently inhabited. Those colonists fled from feudalism; they clung to the <i>alodial</i> land law, and introduced it into Iceland, where it is in force to this day. This <i>odal</i> land tenure did not flow from a sovereign; land was no gift of a master; it was held by no written title, no payment of service; every right was included; the children were all equal heirs, and their rights inalienable, and though they might and often did sell these rights, by repayment in full they might reclaim them. To this day the title of a purchaser of land in Norway is for a given date of a somewhat precarious nature,—a person <i>odal born</i> to the land may return, prove his title, pay the price, and recover from any other holder.

The Icelandic colonists, in order to avoid too large an accumulation in one hand of even the barren lands of Iceland, made a law that each new-comer should only claim the land he could circle by fire—<i>i.e.</i>, cause a man to ride round with a lighted torch—in a day. The chiefs, however, often appropriated a good deal more than that, and settled their crews round them. Each chief consecrated a <i>hof</i> or temple near his own house, and exercised some authority as its priest over the district. The later arrivals were compelled to buy land from others, for then, as now, the habitable lands were small in proportion to the wastes. There were no castles, nor villages; each <i>bondé</i> or landowner made his dwelling independent of the rest, had his cultivated <i>tun</i> and his out-fields.
