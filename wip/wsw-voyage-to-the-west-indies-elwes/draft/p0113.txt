The force of volcanic fire has raised these islands from the sea, but the power of water is continually at work to wear them down, and thus ravines and valleys are made, and at the mouth of them flat sloping plains. These plains have not often been formed in the West Indies, as the sea is usually deep, and has received and swallowed up all that has been carried down from the mountains. Thus, it seems almost possible to judge the age of the island—of course I mean comparatively speaking—by the amount of degradation it has undergone from water. Probably in ages long past the amount of rain that fell was greater than now, though this last autumn a tremendous hurricane, accompanied by a great fall of rain, passed over Guadaloupe and the adjacent islands, destroying an immense deal of property, and causing the loss of several lives. These sudden falls of rain happen occasionally in other countries; for in Basil Hall’s amusing account of Madeira, he tells us how even houses were carried down the great barrancas which run through the town of Funchal, and how on that occasion the torrent filled them to the brim.
