The average Syrian horse is not the Arab steed whose portrait we see in tea-chromos. That style of horse, when he does exist, is found in the Land of Moab, or the desert, and then in a very modified form. For a long, rough journey, such as we were to take, he would be worse than useless. The horses chosen for such a journey are little larger than our own bronchos. They are full of spirit, practically tireless, and as sure-footed as mountain goats.

This sure-footedness is decidedly necessary in traveling through Syria, where one is constantly obliged to ride over steep, pathless mountains or down rocky river-beds. Through all my rough riding, I never saw one of these horses fall. If they ever stumble, it is more likely to be on some smooth, safe road.

I once had to ride up a flight of slippery, moss-grown steps, and then down the other side; and my horse took it as quietly and safely as an American horse would travel his mile in a park bridle-path.

The Arabs and Syrians use saddles that look more like flattened meal-bags than anything else. Their stirrups are shovel-like in shape, and the stirrup-leathers are drawn so short that the rider's knee is on a level with his saddle.
