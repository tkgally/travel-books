I went to sleep in a few moments, and slept until about three o’clock, when I was awakened by something that seemed to brush heavily against the side of the tent near my head; this was followed by a sound as of some one beside me sobbing and sighing. I struck a match, but the tent was empty, except for myself. Looking outside, I saw the guards sleeping beside the dead fire, but no sign of the man or beast who could have brushed my tent. The stars were beginning to pale, and the dawn wind had sprung up. Perhaps this wind had caused the sounds that awoke me. When I went back to my tent, I heard the sobbing sound near me at intervals, but I was too sleepy to notice it long. I have long since given up trying to explain or even to wonder at the unnatural sounds and sensations that belong to a night in the Syrian desert and wilderness. Many things that would, among us, be regarded as unnatural and ghostly occur commonly there, and one becomes used to it all. It is needless to cite the many well-known cases of so-called “supernatural” experiences met with at night in “desert places.” They cease to disturb, after the first night or so. When I awoke again, it was in response to the call of Imbarak the mournful, who came to inform me that it was “<i>six heures et demie</i>, and breakfast was ready.”
