This refusal on the part of a conquered race to be reminded of their departed glory, and their fear of committing sacrilege by some unconscious step upon hallowed ground, struck me at first as rather poetic; so I looked more respectfully at the mourners.

“So they never go there, you say?”

“Well, never unless they are paid to do some sort of work inside the grounds, such as to repair the walls or part of the mosque. They go there in such cases as that, of course, for they are well paid.”

David said this without the least idea of irony, and his statement, as I learned later on, was perfectly correct. When the romance and piety of the ancient Semite run counter to the money-love of the modern Jew, sentiment goes to the wall.

We left the place shortly afterward. As I passed by again on my way to the hotel I saw the line still standing as before; their long dirty coats flapping about their ankles, and the uncut side-locks of hair worn by most of them hanging disheveled and limp.

I recognized several of these disconsolate beings next day in the ghetto, changing money from wire-covered trays filled with coins, or selling merchandise; none looked worse for their melancholy task of the previous afternoon.

On the road home from the Wailing Place, David stopped and pointed to a stone that formed part of an alley wall.

“Do you see that stone with a hollow place something like an open mouth in the middle of it, sir?” he asked.
