There being no hotels in the country, he quarters himself, as a matter of course, on any European he may find, and the latter would look upon it as a slight were he to do otherwise. This arrangement is agreeable to both host and guest, for whilst the latter feels himself at home, the former cannot but find pleasure in seeing a new face and exchanging ideas in a Western tongue. I felt, therefore, no scruples on my arrival at Tabreez in quartering myself for a few days on the amiable family of the Queen’s Consul-General.

In his <i>Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire</i>, Gibbon relates at some length the origin of the once famous Varangian Guard. The term Varangians, or Corsairs, was, he says, first given to bands of adventurers from Denmark, Sweden, and Norway, living by pillage and rapine, who, in the ninth century, offered their services to the descendants of Ruric, and maintained by their valour and ferocity the princes of that house on the throne of Russia. Wladimir the First, finding he no longer required their services, and was not in a position to satisfy their exorbitant demands, persuaded them to seek more remunerative service under a richer master.
