I had always understood that the British Government endeavoured to discourage the consumption of opium among our own subjects. On this point I did not obtain much definite information; but my general impression is that it is to be feared that the use of opium is increasing in India. I heard on high authority that its consumption increases in Burmah, though it is heavily taxed. It must be difficult, if not impossible for the Government, connected as they are with the cultivation of the drug, to prevent its consumption.

The next important item in the Budget is the salt-tax, which from 2,602,670<i>l.</i> in 1858-9 rose to 6,227,301<i>l.</i> in 1874-5. It is argued in support of this tax that it is the only way in which a large majority of the population contribute to the necessities of the state, and that it is only right they should assist the revenue. Objecting to the opium revenue, and sensible of the difficulties which beset the finances of India, I cannot advocate any present alteration which would reduce the amount raised. At the same time it is a great misfortune that we should be obliged to levy a duty which falls on the poorest and most indigent of the population. A government should always strive to avoid taxing the necessaries of life. In this country the working classes pay by their consumption of tea, beer, and spirits, but these articles (unless we except beer) are luxuries and not necessities.
