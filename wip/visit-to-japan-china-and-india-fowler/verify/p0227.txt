This cannot but restrict consumption, and we are told that no salt is used in agriculture, that the cattle are stinted of it, and that the poor along the sea-coast use salt-earth scraped from swamps washed by the sea, while in inland districts the scrapings from saltpetre pans are what thousands of families are reduced to consume. Much disease is said to be engendered by the scarcity of pure salt, and the trade in salt fish is hampered by the duty. It is certainly most unfortunate that we should have to depend for so large a revenue on this necessary of life, but the requirements of our Exchequer seem to leave us no alternative. The only questions are whether a reduction might not maintain the same amount of revenue by leading to increased consumption, and whether improvements might not be made in the mode of collection. As regards the first question, the poverty of the population may make it doubtful whether much can be expected from reduction, though it is a strong argument for it on the ground of humanity. The unequal incidence of the tax as collected in different parts of India has been condemned by Mr. Grant Duff in his account of his journey in <i>Macmillan</i>.[[fnref 7]]
