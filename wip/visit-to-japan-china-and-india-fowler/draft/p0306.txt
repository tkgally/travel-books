At half-past six the train left Suez for Alexandria. It struck me that it would be worth the attention of the Postmaster-General whether the transit through Egypt could not be expedited. When Captain White found he could not save Friday’s train, he reduced his speed, and we had twelve hours’ detention at Suez and six at Alexandria. In our case nearly twenty-four hours might have been saved in the transit of the mail, as we afterwards lost six more at Bologna. Being before our time, this probably was not regarded.

The journey through Egypt was accomplished at a fair speed. In the earlier part of the ride, where the line is near the canal, it was too dark to see anything, but the rising of the moon showed us something of the different channels of the Nile, which we crossed. About 6 a.m. we reached Alexandria, and went on board the “Kashgar,” Captain Baker. I had not been in Alexandria for twenty-six years, but as it was Sunday morning I did not like to be sight-seeing. I therefore contented myself with viewing the imposing city and Pompey’s Pillar from the sea, as well as Pharos, within which our ship was lying. At noon we started, and about two lost sight of Pharos, the last object which remained visible.
