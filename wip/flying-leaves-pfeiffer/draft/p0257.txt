Withal I contrived to join in one long drive before we left, that which is here known as the “round trip.” It is a tour right through the valley, down to the cascades at its south-western end. We took the right bank of the river from Barnard’s in going, and returned by the left, having the great advantage of Mr. Hutchings’ company by the way. We got out at a small encampment of “Digger” Indians—so called from their living not so much by hunting or fishing as upon roots. They count among the lowest of the aboriginal inhabitants, their way of life being little calculated to develop intelligence. Their huts, consisting of a few converging planks, are formed with less art than the nests of most birds. On the other hand, their utensils—culinary and other—exhibit considerable ingenuity. We saw bowls of reeds so closely woven that they were completely water-tight. An old woman was the sole guardian of this primitive hamlet at the time of our visit. She was squatting on the ground, rubbing away with a brush made of the fibrous stem of some native plant, at one of the plaited bowls, and looked up as we approached with a not unkindly glance. Dressed in rags and begrimed with dirt, it was a sordid life for a human creature to be enduring.
