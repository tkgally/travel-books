# Leaf X.

WE leave Salt Lake City on a morning keen and bright, the train passing by, sometimes cleaving, the skirts of the Great Salt Lake; altogether a treeless and a desolate-looking region, in spite of the name of Paradise which is so freely bestowed upon it. A night upon the train has brought us to another day.

On, and still on, we journey to the West. The dawn breaks clear; not a cloud in the heavens, so far as they are visible to me. The sky has the look of a liquid, not wholly transparent—some imperfectly mingled, ethereal milk and wine. All below is prairie, with conically shaped hillocks, which might be the tents of some Brobdingnagian savage tribe in the distance. As I watch, the dawn brightens into day. The dun, yellowish prairie is now bordered by vaster rugged hills, whose hollows are filled with the blue breath of the morning. Now no more hills, only a rolling ocean of prairie, which seems to have swallowed up the world: but it has not; it is, on the contrary, the tide of civilization which is for ever pressing upon and narrowing its borders.
