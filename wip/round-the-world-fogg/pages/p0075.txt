Before we reach the hotel we meet several coolies propelling heavy two-wheeled carts loaded with boxes of tea, or sugar and rice in sacks, marking time with a laud monotonous cry, <i>whang-hai! whang-hai!</i> Other porters are bearing merchandize to and from the quay, each pair with a burden between them on a pole, others singly, with baskets slung on each end of a pole about six feet long, which rests upon the shoulder. These porters are very mus ular and healthy-looking fellows, although their diet is entirely fish and rice, both of which are very cheap, and of excellent quality.

The first word which I have learned in the Japanese vocabulary is one easily remembered, and reminds me of home. The ordinary salutation is “<i>Ohaio</i>,” signifying “good day.” When uttered by a “Jap” it is always accompanied with a graceful inclination of the body, in strong contrast with the slight bob of the head with which Jones, Smith and Brown jerk out “How are you?” The parting word is “<i>Saionara</i>,” which loses nothing in softness by contrast with the French <i>adieu</i>, or the Italian <i>addio</i>, and the elaborate courtesy of all classes in Japan makes a very favorable impression on the stranger.
