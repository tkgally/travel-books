The donkeys appear to be of the same frame of mind, as they certainly object and rarely can be driven by the lines. A boy must walk by their heads or a bolt or a sit down—generally the latter—would ensue every minute or so. To-day the beast drawing Miss P——’s rickshaw manages to separate itself from the vehicle and turning around gravely regards her with a “What are <i>you</i> going to do about it?” expression, all the time rousing the echoes and the neighbouring lions in the Zoo by its loud-mouthed protests at the indignity being thrust upon it. Reaching the landing stage we find our boy with the saddles awaiting us and a snub-nosed ferry-boat shortly puffs up to the pier.

From here one has the junction of the rivers spread out before one and can plainly discern the line, extending out of sight to the northward, where the clear waters of the Blue reach the muddy currents of the White Nile. The main river stretches away to the northward, broad and calm, and in the delta formed just here is the island of Tuti, flooded during high water times. Behind us the White Nile spreads off towards the equator, lake-like and placid, being some four miles in width not far to the southward.
