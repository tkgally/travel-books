He is therefore selected, and in company with “Lucrezia Borgia,” and “Osiris,” carries us swiftly past the Esbekiya Gardens, through the shadowy “Mousky,” where stray sunbeams light up gorgeous colours and the balmy air is laden with attar of roses. Now a long string of camels just in from Arabia, now a whole harem mounted on donkeys and guarded by a repulsive-looking eunuch, pass us by; and now a funeral procession, with its strange screech-owl cry of mourning.

Through a shadowy doorway comes the swish, swish, of swiftly moving garments, and the uncertain light shows us the ghostly figures of the dancing dervishes fluttering round and round like great white moths.

As we leave the streets and mount to the citadel, the Khedive, a pleasant-faced man of middle life, dressed in black and wearing the “fez,” passes us in an open landau, preceded by two running footmen dressed in flowing white garments, with velvet jackets embroidered in gold, and carrying long wands in their hands. Softly on the evening air come the slow, solemn notes of an English funeral march, and we stand reverently, with heads uncovered, as they bear past us all that is left of some poor soldier boy who will never see Merry England any more.
