The name, as a rule, is beyond their comprehension, and when the name is given without the number amusing mistakes sometimes occur, the consequences of which a quick-tempered passenger or officer is apt to visit upon the head of the meek and inoffensive waiter. The Englishman or American abroad too often forgets to make allowance for the difficulties to foreigners of his own speech, which are to no race more insurmountable than to the Mongol, and—a monoglot himself—is apt to be impatient at the linguistic ignorance of other nations. That English will one day be the universal speech may be granted, but the world is not yet come to that. Meanwhile, many of our countrymen abroad will act as if it had, and rail at the stupidity of the Asiatic and the African, no less than the European, who does not speak their language.

My first passage across the Pacific was of the pleasantest, and the second was like unto it. Indeed, I don't know whether it would be possible to find the North Atlantic for thirteen days so much like the proverbial mill-pond, and then a few weeks later to find it again in the like placid mood for a similar period.
