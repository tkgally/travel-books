Two miles out of Nikkô I was overtaken by one of the guests of the Kanaya, Mr. Guinness of Dublin, who was touring in Japan on his way home from Burma. Leaving his <i>kuruma</i> (which was drawn by two coolies), he accompanied me on foot the rest of the stiff journey to Chûzenji. While the coolies followed the zigzag windings of the road, we ascended by the steeper short-cuts usually taken by pedestrians, arriving at a tea-house—a recognised stopping-place—halfway up the hill some time in advance of the <i>kuruma</i>. Of all hill roads perhaps those of Japan have the easiest gradient. When you have walked a mile or two up one of these roads, you are very little nearer your objective, as the stork flies, than when you set out. The extent of ground traversed is out of all proportion to the real ascent made. Hence a foot-path more or less defined cuts across these tortuous windings, and good climbers usually take the more direct and steeper way, and gain in time what they lose in comfort. <i>Kagos</i>, too, are generally taken along the shorter routes.
