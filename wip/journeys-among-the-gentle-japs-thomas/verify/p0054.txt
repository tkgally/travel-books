An excellent institution in the foreign settlement is the Seamen’s Mission Room, under the charge of the Rev. W. T. Austin, the Chaplain. The reading-room is the best I have come upon in any foreign port, far or near, and must prove a boon to our seamen which they do not often find. I turned in every day during my stay in the port (for, though not a seaman, I was assured of a welcome), and always found it well patronised.

The Japanese currency, strange as the money seems at first, is soon mastered. It is on the decimal system, and consists of the <i>yen</i> as the unit (which, at par, corresponds to the American dollar), the <i>sen</i> (or cent, the hundredth part of a <i>yen</i>), and the <i>rin</i> (the tenth of a <i>sen</i>). There are paper notes of 1, 5, and 10 <i>yen</i> and upward, as well as of 20 <i>sen</i> and 50 <i>sen</i>. Gold is practically never seen. The silver pieces are 1 <i>yen</i>, 50 <i>sen</i>, 20 <i>sen</i>, 10 <i>sen</i>, and 5 <i>sen</i>. There is also a nickel piece of 5 <i>sen</i>. The copper pieces are 2 <i>sen</i>, 1 <i>sen</i>, 5 <i>rin</i>, and 1 <i>rin</i>. The <i>rin</i> is a peculiar-looking coin, having a square hole in the centre.
