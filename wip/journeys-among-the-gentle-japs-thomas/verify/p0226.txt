Perhaps it was not till she observed my painful awkwardness that it occurred to her that I was a stranger to the ways of her country. She had possibly had Europeans at her house with whom it was a matter of indifference as to whether they sat, like a Christian, or squatted, like a Buddhist or a Mohammedan. Once in the semi-European room, I was again at my ease, and had the rare advantage of being fanned while eating by two waiting-maids, assisted at intervals by the hostess, who, seeing that I was covered with perspiration (for we were in the hottest part of Japan), thoughtfully undid my collar, and sent some cooling currents down my chest. (<i>À propos</i> of the hot weather, I heard that during that summer a large firm in Tôkyô fined each one of its <i>employés</i> who complained of the heat a <i>sen</i>, because their brothers in Korea had greater heat to endure and bore it patiently. The money went to a fund for the benefit of those who were invalided home from the war.)

The meal, including the fanning and the share in the <i>geisha</i> entertainment, cost me sevenpence.
