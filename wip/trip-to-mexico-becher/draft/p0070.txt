Some idea of the magnitude of the pulque trade may be inferred, from the fact, that there is a daily train on the railway between Mexico, Puebla and Vera Cruz, used exclusively in its transport all the year round. But the use of the maguey does not end with pulque; a spirit is distilled from pulque, in taste something between a pleasing liqueur and Scotch whiskey; good vinegar is made from stale pulque; the thick roots of the maguey are used by the Indians in place of soap; brushes are made from the base of its prickly leaves; twine and paper from its fibres; the dry parts of the leaves serve as hones for sharpening razors: textures are made from its filaments, and its sprouts when young, are eaten half roasted. I learn from the Spanish book about the railway that “with justice Father Acosta gave the maguey the name of the ‘miraculous plant.’”[[fnref *]] At half-past one we stop at Apizaco, but long before reaching it Popocatapetl and Iztaccihuatl have been in view—solemn and grand, their icy peaks glistening in the sun.

[[fn *]] I have omitted to notice the town of Huamantla, which we passed about an hour before reaching Apizaco, probably because we were engrossed with Malintzi, which is close to it.
