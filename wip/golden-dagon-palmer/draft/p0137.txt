Shway-Dagoung, the Golden Dagon—an octagonal pagoda of solid masonry, without an opening, holding up its jingling coronal even with the spire of the proud St. Paul’s, gilded from base to pinnacle, tarnishing in the rains of many wet, and glaring in the suns of many dry seasons—formed the centre of an area of fortification four miles square, and planned in this wise: The outer lines were marked by a stockade, having four equal sides, of four miles each, one side fronting on the river. Many small batteries flanked this at different points; and the ground between it and the river, and on both sides, bristled with such vegetable bayonets as those we found at Kemmendine—short flinty bamboos, planted thickly, their sharpened points projecting some six or eight inches. These were for the benefit of storming parties. Beside these, mines of gunpowder were discovered, ready to be sprung by means of trains conducted into the jungle.
