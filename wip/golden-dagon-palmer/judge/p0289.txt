She crouched in her death-fear; she had made no sign, betrayed no curiosity. The Burmese woman is too well trained to make her existence apparent in the presence of men, unless she is called for. There she had been hiding, stifling her baby between her breasts to smother its small cry, and, like myself, wondering what would come next. I crept in on my hands and knees, and we squatted face to face. To me, she was an angel—the realization of all that is beautiful in heaven. To her, I was hell—a black, fiendish thing, of which she had a superstitious horror as frightful as it was vague. I was a something that was cruel, something that killed, something that fired great guns and made infernal noises and ghastly gashes, something that, wherever it went, spread death and flames before it, and left ashes and dead bodies behind.
