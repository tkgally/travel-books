Sometimes, it is true, he picks it up in several pieces, and many a family arriving at San Francisco have had their opinion of the convenience of the American system sorely modified as they stood by the wreck of their baggage. An American railway porter treats each individual piece of baggage as if he owed it a personal grudge. Easy as it may seem to take the lightest and frailest boxes as the basis of a pile, and then bring down upon them the sharp, iron-bound edges of a Saratoga trunk, it requires a good deal of skill and practice so to deal with whole carloads of luggage. Yet I have never seen at any station along four thousand miles of railway a single instance of failure. An English railway porter handles baggage with comparative kindliness, for it represents to him sixpence or a shilling. Tipping not being the practice in America, the railway porter has nothing to look for or to hope for, and accordingly takes it out of the baggage.
