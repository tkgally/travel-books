We contented ourselves with the slower but safer “gharry,” and drove to Mân-Mandil ghât, where we took boat, an over-decked top-heavy structure rowed by four men with oars having the maximum of loom and the minimum of blade. In addition to the four men who rowed there was one who shouted a good deal and steered a little, and two who did nothing till we disembarked, when they asked for backsheesh.

It was eight o’clock, an hour at which bathing is in full swing. All along the town-side of the river ghâts (stone steps) run down into the water. Generally there is a temple at the top of the ghât. By the Sindhia ghât are two lofty turrets, which appear to be toppling to the ground. The foundations, built almost in the river, have sunk, and it is said are still sinking. The veil of a temple close by has been rent in twain, and some day there will be shrieking and sudden death when these massive turrets complete their destiny and fall with a crash among the crowd always encircling them on its way to and from the river. Lower down, a palace once belonging to the Rajah of Gwalior has sunk into the earth, only the ruined and roofless walls of its upper stories uplifted from the tomb.
