All of us who are leaving the <i>Ceylon</i> at San Francisco are commencing to pack, and a greater accent is given to the ordinary humdrum nature of ship-life, from the knowledge that we shall so soon be leaving our friends and the old ship.

[[right]] April 25th.

Since the 20th we have had very calm weather, but an immense swell. This morning a large full-rigged ship passed about five miles from us—the first ship we have sighted at sea since leaving Japan. Last night we rolled about in a fearful way. It is said there must have been a heavy gale somewhere north of us. The waves are immense, being about a hundred yards apart—glassy and without a ripple, for the air is absolutely breezeless! It is almost impossible to sit about the deck, and to keep in one's berth at night is also a matter of the greatest difficulty. To-night is most glorious, with a clear starlit sky, and a soft radiant moon.

[[right]] April 26th, 1882.

This is our last day on the <i>Ceylon</i>. The immense waves have almost gone, and the morning is lovely, with a freshening breeze. At noon we are under full sail, with a spanking beam-wind. The ship is now rattling along quicker than at any time since leaving Southampton. We are doing over thirteen knots an hour, and it is said we shall be in at six p.m.
