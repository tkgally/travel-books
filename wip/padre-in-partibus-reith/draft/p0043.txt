Van der Tuuk attended, but distracted the attention of his neighbours, and upset their gravity, by thrusting on their notice a pair of stockings he had put on in honour of the occasion. The <i>dominie</i> and the missionary are unknown in Bali, and the Dutch are not apostles of Christianity to the natives. Some time ago I was in another part of Netherlands India, which I shall not more particularly mention, where the <i>dominie</i> is better known than here. I was presented to the Resident, who said he would be glad if I would give a <i>dienst</i> (service) while I was there. “I will gif you” he said, “de verandah of de Residence, and you shall gif—how call you it? a speech—a sermon? Zoo, I call it speech. You shall cast your—what is it?—your line, and you shall catch first B. and zen de Doctor, and zen perhaps me. You shall tunder fort de lightnings of your eloquence and do us goot. We need to be made goot here. We are all very loose.”

I ventured to remind him that there were already <i>dominies</i> in the neighbourhood.

“Ach ja,” he replied, “but dey are all very loose too!”

Van der Tuuk died in Sourabaya towards the end of 1894. I was anxious to see the house where he lived in Bali, and made enquiries of the natives on the roads about Singaradja, but the mention of his name seemed to waken no memories. At last our guide asking information from a passing woman, tried an imitation of the deep, harsh voice of the hermit. A gleam of intelligence shot across the woman's face. “Ah-h-h!” she cried, “<i>Sini, Tuan, sini</i>!” [[fnref *]] and pointed to a village close by. We entered and found the house, partly dismantled, but there was enough to show the kind of abode it had been.
