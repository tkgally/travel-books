The bathrooms in the Dutch hotels are is general large, airy, well-lit, and plentifully supplied with water, and the Colonial Dutchman is extremely fond of splashing about therein. You never meet a Dutchman who is afraid of personal cleanliness, like the Frenchman who said “I vash my hands vonce a day, my face vonce a week, but my feet <i>nevare</i>!” The sound of splashing water with a whistling obligato (only two airs are known in Java, “Daisy” and the “Lorelei”) is heard all day from the bathrooms in the hotels. In some other respects, more cleanliness is necessary; the colonial Dutchman either must have no sense of smell or enjoy odours that sicken and disgust other people. The sanitary arrangements of the Dutch hotels in the East are without exception the worst possible. His morning ablutions over, the Dutchman dons a fresh suit of pyjamas, and comes to the hotel table for a light meal which he calls <i>ontbijt</i>, which lies on the table from 7-30 till 10 a.m. <i>Hors d’œuvres</i> of various kinds, cold meat, eggs, fruit, tea, coffee or claret are the materials of which this meal is composed. It is much the same as the Anglo-Indian <i>chota hazri</i>, but if the stranger does not wish to have mere scraps to eat, he must take care to come to the table early. If he has business to do, the Dutchman gets into white clothes after this meal, and goes to his work till noon. If he has none, he loafs about the hotel or lies at full length in his chair till the hotel boys bring forward the <i>pahits</i>,[[fnref *]] an hour before <i>rijsttafel</i>. Between noon and the tiffin hour the guests assemble gradually in the front part of the hotel, and pass the time in drinking <i>pahits</i>, most of them doing a large import business in this way. Then comes <i>rijsttafel</i>, the event of the day. I confess to the seductiveness of this famous dish, or rather succession of dishes—at first; but before long it not only palls on a well-educated appetite, but becomes positively revolting, to a man with a liver at least.
