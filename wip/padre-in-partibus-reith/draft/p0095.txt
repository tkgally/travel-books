The defiant crows of the imprisoned warriors are sometimes absolutely deafening. The rajahs and chiefs live in houses built in a more ambitious style than those of the common people, brick being used instead of mud, and the surrounding walls being much higher and graced with ornamental copings. Every village lies under the shade of a rich green canopy, the trees being chiefly banyans, far away up in which can be descried rude platforms and shelters of bamboo where, we were told, sharpshooters are posted in time of war.

Returning to the Towkay, we found him engaged in a solemn function, which his servant informed us was a <i>bichara</i>,[[fnref *]] a <i>preksa wang</i>.[[fnref †]] From the courtyard we saw him seated on a high chair with the profound air of a Lord Chancellor; plaintiff and defendant were invisible to us, but one of them was a woman whose voice, like Tennyson’s brook, went on for ever, without break, without rise or fall,—one long, monotonous, but not unmusical ripple. The pleadings of the other side were heard as an accompaniment, in a harsh masculine tone. At last the judgment was given. It took ten minutes to deliver, with frequent pauses. It was divided by the pauses into short paragraphs, and each paragraph ended in a loud-voiced

“<i>Chee-laka, bee-natang punya orang lu!</i>[[fnref ‡]] <i>U-u-ugh!</i>”

I could not discover whether the man or the woman was addressed in these complimentary terms. Perhaps it was both. The trial over, we were told that the rajah would receive us at 8 p.m. in his palace, and as this would involve spending the night at Gyanyar we made enquiry about sleeping accommodation.
