The passengers on the <i>Hecate</i> watched the river intently to catch sight of the celebrated corpses which are said to float down on the tide in countless thousands. They were hugely disappointed, for they saw only one corpse in twenty miles—the mortal remains of a dog floating on its back distended and horrible. Soon the native villages on the river-banks grew closer to one another, with a tendency to become one continuous line. Later, pinnacles and masts appeared over the low vegetation in front, and we knew that Bangkok was near. The suggestion that the passengers should take a dose of “cholera-mixture” before reaching the infected port was welcomed by all, and a boy was commissioned to prepare the drug. The concoction goes by another name in Singapore, and those who have tasted it know that it is certain to kill the choleraic germs, and also the person who has harboured them. Threading its way carefully through the main street of Bangkok the <i>Hecate</i> reached a wharf in due time. The main street of Bangkok is the river Menam, from which at frequent intervals, little canals, called <i>khlongs</i>, branch off on both sides. Some are about the same breadth as the Stamford Road Canal in Singapore, but, being shut in by native houses, their odour is infinitely worse. Bangkok is well called the Venice of the East, for its thoroughfares are chiefly waterways—the river and the <i>khlongs</i>—and these are crowded with native craft of all sorts and sizes, with boats, with steam-launches that go shrieking and pooping in all directions, like bluebottles buzzing in the sunshine. The river is a sight never to be forgotten. Moored at wharves, or anchored in midstream, lie great steamers and sailing ships, men-of-war and gunboats, with an air of reposeful dignity amid a restless swarm of launches, boats, sampans and canoes that shoot hither and thither, in perpetual motion and with an appearance of frantic hurry.
