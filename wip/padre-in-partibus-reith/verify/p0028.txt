Having thus seen the last of Singapore for a time, I turned my chair, and forgetting the things which were behind, turned my thoughts to the things that are before—to the new and strange peoples it will shortly be my lot to see. One has read of them in books, and heard of them from travellers, but to see them with one’s own eyes is to get impressions which no book or narrative can give. The traveller brings you only the stone of the fruit he has eaten, you get the fruit itself by going for it. It is strange to reflect that this vast archipelago, with a population of some forty or fifty millions, containing at least three utterly distinct races (Europeans excluded), was not so much as heard of when Church Councils were settling with dogmatic precision, the nature, needs, and destiny of all mankind! What might have been the effect on Christian anthropology if SS. Athanasius and Augustine could have taken a trip to Java, or if Luther, Calvin and Knox had been set down “where Chinese drive” in their human ant-hills?

Friday morning found us off Pulau Panjang, a small island east of Bintang, and the afternoon saw us across the Equator, passing from Spring to Autumn in a few seconds, without the intervention of Summer. We were suffered to cross the line without a visit from Father Neptune, who has grown sulky at the introduction of steam navigation and refuses to pay his usual call of ceremony on steamers. We were out of sight of land till the next morning when we sighted Serutu and the Karimata Islands. Serutu, where the <i>Nam Yong</i> came to grief last year (1895) consists of a mountain of three or four peaks rising abruptly from the sea, and wooded to the top. There is no sign of population, but through the telescope could be seen little bays with sandy beaches where one would naturally expect to find a Malay <i>kampong</i> in a clump of cocoanut palms.
