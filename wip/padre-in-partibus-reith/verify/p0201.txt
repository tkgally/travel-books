For example, <i>ouw</i> is the English <i>ow</i>, <i>nieuw</i> (four vowels) is the English <i>new</i> (as pronounced in Glasgow), and combinations such as <i>aaie</i> are far from rare. Another peculiarity is the combination <i>oe</i> for the long <i>u</i> sound. Voltaire used to say that English was only French badly pronounced, and one might say that Dutch is only Scotch badly spelled, with equal truth. I once had a Scotch landlady the spelling of whose weekly bills was a pleasure to look forward to from Sunday to Saturday, and a joy till the next was due. She was uncertain as to the true spelling of the word sausages, and tried a new one every week, ultimately achieving, as I thought, every possible combination of letters that could represent the sound of the word, from the elementary <i>sassiges</i> to the highly developed <i>sauceadges</i>. But the ingenuity of Dutch orthographers had long before out-distanced that of my landlady, and evolved a spelling before which her efforts pale into insignificance—<i>saucijsjes!</i> Until one grows accustomed to the sight of an unfamiliar language on signs, notices, and advertisements, it seems ludicrous and childish, especially those words of it which are almost the same as corresponding words in English. Take the English word <i>line</i>, for example, as applied to a fleet of steamers. You get a shock when you see it on a German advertisement as <i>Linie</i>: it looks like baby-talk. The Swedish spell <i>cigarette</i> in so original a manner that though everybody in Singapore knows the word, there are few who could point it out. Dutch, like Norwegian, looks unusually <i>bizarre</i> and ludicrous to the Englishman, chiefly through the peculiar value and position assigned to the letter <i>j</i>, and the odd combinations of vowels. The feeling I have just described, the sense of the ludicrousness and childishness of an unfamiliar language, is probably due to the power of association.
