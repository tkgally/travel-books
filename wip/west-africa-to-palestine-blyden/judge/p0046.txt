There has been a certain attempt to put like things with like, either in sense or in sound, or, if possible, both. When the sound is alike, the object is partly, I suppose, to contrast homonymous words, as in the instance I gave above, in which <i>(khalk)</i> is distinguished from <i>(khulk)</i>, and which may be, not quite literally, rendered

<verse>
A fair nation.
A fine nature.
A free tongue—(Lit. facunda oratio)
Excellent of speech,
</verse>

The fourth line is thrown in to finish off the third, which has nothing to do with the first two. This is very manifest in the middle stanza of the page before (p. 3):—

<verse>
A beautiful face.
An eye tinted with kohl.
Umbrageous shade.
Over man and beast.
</verse>

“Umbrageous shade” is meant for <i>(zillun zalilun)</i> which Freitag expressly gives in his smaller dictionary “multa vel perpetua umbra.” I admit, by the way, that my translation (from Swift’s <i>Art of Sinking</i>, I think) implies a false criticism. <i>Umbrageous shade</i> is a tautology, because <i>umbrageous</i> pretends to be something different from <i>shady</i>, and is not; whereas if you say plainly <i>shady shade</i>, you will be understood to double the word for intensification, just as in English we might say <i>shade of shades</i>. However, the translation “perpetua” shows that Freitag or the author of the Kamus thought <i>(zalil)</i> might come from the other tense of the root, that of permanence. If I am right in the character I have attributed to the paper, it is probable that the phrases would many of them be very idiomatic, and that one could not make them out from such a poor lexicon as the small Freitag, still less from my knowledge of the language.
