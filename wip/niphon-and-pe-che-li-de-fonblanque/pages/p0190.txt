Let them bow their heads in the dust before a Yaconin as they may, it is less an act of servile submission than a courtesy exacted by usage and a duty owing to superior authority. Freedom is older than despotism; and centuries of arbitrary government have not extinguished its spark in the breast of the Japanese peasant. Those well-built, muscular men, who stand erect at their doors, holding their little children by the hand, have a sense of liberty and self-respect never to be met with in a race of slaves or cowards; those laughing women beside them know and appreciate the sacred happiness of the domestic hearth; even the little children (and nowhere do you meet this true indication of material prosperity—troops of merry, rosy-cheeked children—to a greater extent), even they do not crouch before the foreigner, though, doubtless, for many a day to come, they will, in their naughty moods, be threatened with the most terrible of Bogies—the <i>Tojins</i>.
