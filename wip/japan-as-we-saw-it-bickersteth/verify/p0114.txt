Therefore, parents of the upper middle class, being increasingly anxious to have their daughters not only well educated, but able to talk to a foreign visitor, will often allow them to attend a mission school, in spite of the stipulation that regular Christian instruction will be included in the school course.

S. Hilda’s School has about forty pupils, and is divided into four departments. I. <i>The Jingo Sho Gakko</i>, or Kindergarten. II. <i>The Koto Sho Gakko</i>, or Upper Kindergarten. III. <i>The Chu Gakko</i>, or Middle School. IV. <i>The College Class</i>. This arrangement is in strict accordance with that of an ordinary Japanese school.

I. The Jingo Sho Gakko, or Kindergarten. In order to use this name, a teacher certificated by Government has to be employed, and the school is then recognized and examined by Government officials. The course is as follows: Reading, writing, arithmetic (English and Japanese), manners, morals, and English, the teachers being Otoke San, Takida San, and Miss Snowden. When Miss Snowden mentioned “Morals,” I wondered what the pretty party of babies I had seen in the school had to do with so serious a subject; but she soon relieved my anxiety.
