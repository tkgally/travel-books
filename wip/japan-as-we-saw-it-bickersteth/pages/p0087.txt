But to return to the intermingling of the two ancient creeds in the minds of the Japanese. Two good reasons can be given for the apparent puzzle. (1): Buddhism, which entered Japan by way of Korea (556 <sc>a.d.</sc>), gave its immediate sanction to Shintoism, and admitted the gods of Shinto within its pantheon. (2): Shintoism, a vague ancestor-worship, with scarcely any services and no dogmas, left the people free to adopt all the elaborate system and ritual of Buddhism without any break with their own past.

After lunch in a tea-house on the edge of the ravine, we wandered down the village street, if street it could be called, being merely some irregular flights of steps, and now and then an arched bridge of scarlet lacquer, a delightful bit of colour among the surroundings of dark cryptomeria and rocks. We visited a Buddhist priest’s house, where my brother had lived during part of a summer holiday, and returned to Ikao late in the afternoon, climbing once more to the plateau, and passing the beautiful lake on the summit of the pass.
