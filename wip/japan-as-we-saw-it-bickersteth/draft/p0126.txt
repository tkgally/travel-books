They are in every sense representative of the active life of modern Japan, not of the small clique of nobles who have lived in more or less retirement since the Restoration, nor of the peasants, with their slower intelligence; but of the <i>samurai</i> (the scholars and soldiers of the old <i>régime</i>), and the <i>farmers</i>, who together form the powerful middle class of the present Empire.

It is scarcely surprising, therefore, that Mr. Moore was able to tell us of one boy who pulled a jinriksha each evening, and of another who sold newspapers daily from 4 to 7 <sc>a.m.</sc>, in order to earn the necessary school fees.

About twenty-eight students are now Christians, and attend very regularly the daily Evensong in the Mission House Chapel, and the early Celebration of Holy Communion on Sundays. Non-Christians come in increasing numbers to the instruction classes which are held by the missionaries, some from curiosity, others from a real dissatisfaction with their own creeds.

I shall not easily forget the bright face of one boy when I asked him if he was a catechumen, and he answered, with much emphasis, in English, “No; but I <i>want</i> to be!”
