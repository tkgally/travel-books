We dashed down the sharp zigzag path into valley, our men having certainly no pity for ladies’ nerves! One acted as a drag, and the other, rushing ahead, pulled with all his might, as if about to throw himself, his light machine, and its occupant, over the edge of the precipice. But, no! Just as we drew breath in preparation for the impending accident, they slowed down to a trot that exactly swung us round the dangerous curve, and I, who was in front, had the enjoyment of watching how my next neighbor endured each ordeal, until by a final rush we were again in the valley, and could watch our panting runners as they washed their hands and faces in a little mountain stream before taking us down the long winding road to Nikko. (N.B.—Jinriksha men are, or should be, strict teetotalers, as they find stimulants shortcn their lives.)

We intended to visit the temples of Iyemitsu, Iyeyasu’s grandson, next morning, but we were prevented by a typhoon, a somewhat severe one; which made going out a sheer impossibility. However, by 2 p.m. the torrents of rain ceased, and Mrs. Bickersteth and I started in jinrikshas to do a little shopping in Nikko, my father preceding us on foot.
