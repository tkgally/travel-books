“Arriving at Osaka we made for the Naniwa, the scene of the terrible disaster, which rumor, with its wonted exaggeration, had magnified into 300 killed. Fortunately it was only tenth of that number who were thus suddenly hurried into eternity, but the catastrophe was none the less appalling. En route one could notice that almost every solid house had sustained more or less damage. Telegraph poles were out of the perpendicular, walls cracked, chimneys serrated, and leaning at peculiar angles. One big smoke-stack near the Naniwa Mill was frightfully cracked and disjointed; but still stood, though in a very precarious pofition.

“The road to the mill as we neared if was thronged with spectators coming from, or going to, the scene of the disaster. Some were relatives, whose cheeks and eyes betrayed their loss, while all spoke in awed tones, remarkably contrasting with Japanese wonted vivacity. The view from the bend in the road where we first caught sight of the mill was one of desolation. The roof had disappeared, and jagged portions of the walls stood tottering.
