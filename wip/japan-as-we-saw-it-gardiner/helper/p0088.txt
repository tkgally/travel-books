# Kobe.

[[image: Harbor and foreign waterfront of Kobe with ships and mountains behind]]

K<sc>obe</sc>, the commercial rival of Yokohama, and one of the five treaty ports, is a city of one hundred and thirty-five thousand inhabitants; and while the proportion of resident foreigners is not so great as in the last named place, foreign ideas and language seem to have taken root and spread to a greater extent. This is evidenced by the fact that more or less English is spoken in almost every Japanese shop here.

The situation of the city is very fine. From the edge of the really beautiful harbor, along which it is built, for nearly three miles is a level a quarter of a mile in width, upon which the foreign settlement is built; and from thence a gradual slope, on which are the residences, is maintained to the foot of the rugged but picturesque chain of mountains, which form the background and reach a height of two thousand five hundred feet. Even the face of this range is dotted with fine foreign houses.

To the west of the “settlement” lies the Japanese native town, extending to the Minatogawa (<i>Mee-nah-toh-gah-wah</i>) River, dividing Hiogo (<i>Hyoh-goh</i>) from Kobe. The banks of the river are lined with pine trees, along which are well-kept walks leading to several temples and <i>one more</i> Dai-Butsu.

The principal bridge connecting the two cities is at the end of Moto-Machi, the leading business street of the native town. In this street curio shops abound; but that known as “Hari-shin’s” contains more <i>old</i> stock than any one establishment in the country, from any desired kind of a god to suits of armor, pagodas, stone lanterns, swords, old and new porcelains, embroideries, lacquers, copper and bronzes.
