Tokitsu boasts of two very poor inns, that nearest the water being perhaps the least objectionable. Within the inn, near the hatoba, the “ticket agent,” with blank forms printed on rice-paper, and a brush and India ink to fill them up, sat upon a raised open space, while he dispensed tickets for the various points, the rate to all places seeming to be the same—twenty-five sen.

At nine o’clock in the morning, in an open sampan, we were taken out to the steamer lying at anchor,—and such a steamer! About the size of one of our smallest tug-boats, the “first-class saloon” being only four feet high, compelling us to follow the example of our Japanese fellow passengers and “squat” upon the floor, which, however, was covered with a matting. Of course every one smoked, and some of the odors coming from the diminutive pipe-bowls excited our wonder if not our admiration.

[[image: narrow road curving between steep grassy banks with a lone traveller walking toward Tokitsu]]

As we were the only foreigners on board we made friends with the Japanese captain, and obtained permission for Mrs. G. to sit in the wheel-house, while the writer occupied the little deck in front of it.

The Gulf is a most picturesque sheet of water, its surface dotted with grass and pine clad islands, while the mainland is generally cultivated, excepting where mountain ranges rise from the shores.

The village of Omura is twelve miles from Tokitsu, and Sonogi, the second stopping place, twenty-seven miles. Here we go ashore in a sampan, and at the Matsumori <i>(Mah-tsoo-moh-ree)</i> inn secure jinrikishas (two coolies each) for the journey to Ureshino <i>(Oo-ray-shee-noh)</i>, eight miles, and Arita <i>(Ah-ree-tah)</i>, eight miles farther on. The road is very good and quite pretty to Ureshino, celebrated for its natural hot-baths; and while an investigation of the latter is making, Mrs. G. is cared for by the Japanese women in the Shiwoya <i>(Shee-woh-yah)</i> inn.
