About this Mexican country there is a desolate grandeur, something irredeemable and untamable. It matches its barbarity with your own; it calls out the wild and free and speaks to the suppressed in you; to that which the life of polite trifling has for so long dwarfed and obscured. You acquire the “Mexican habit” which is a kind of lotus-eating. You understand why people return year after year, how eventually they are absorbed by it. The mind is fitted to distance, to large perspectives, to a remote horizon. Green cane-fields of the bottom lands accentuate the grim aspect of mountains and cliffs, so dominant, forceful and rugged. Stern and unbending nopals and organos replace the soft outlines of maple and birch. As if to atone for this severity, the land is enveloped in light, softer and more yielding than elsewhere, and charged with more delicate gradations of colour. It is desolate, but it is a beautiful desolation; it is sad, but you love it for its sadness.
