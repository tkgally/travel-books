Well, morning comes to us at last, as we toss wakefully on the soft mattresses, which seem to us stuffed with hot coals. At Poughkeepsie, on the Hudson River, we make a light breakfast of milk, peaches, and delicate corn-bread; at half-past ten we arrive in New York City. And, when we get there, we wish ourselves back on the shores of Lake Ontario, or at Niagara, or in the train again, anywhere—in the bewildering maze of the Buffalo dépôt, even—anywhere but where we are. For New York seizes us, the moment we set foot on its glaring pavements, in the grip of its terrible heat—the stifling city heat, so much more baleful than any tropical temperature by riverside, lake, hill, or forest.

A friend has come to meet us; he looks flaccid and limp and half-melted, in spite of his alpaca coat and cool white straw hat. He seems glad to see us, but I fear it is less from affection than because he has reached that stage of suffering at which it becomes a comfort to hail fellow-victims.

‘Well, Jack, how is New York?’ we inquire.
