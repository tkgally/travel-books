We next inspect a comparatively light and airy cell, with a narrow grating high up, to our eyes unattainable and impassable, but through which the Indian chief ‘Wild Cat’ is said to have effected his escape. The great Osceola, his companion in obscurity, nobly refused to avail himself of the same means. It strikes us as possible that the ‘Cat’ was the slenderer and more agile of the two. From the fort we cross a rough and pathless stretch of sand and turf to another relic of the past—to the old city gates. They are built of coquina, of course. We inspect the barred and grated sentinel-boxes, the high towers flanking the gateway, and dutifully resist the temptation to chip off a piece of coquina as a souvenir.

The next day is Easter Sunday; the quaint old streets are crowded with gaily-dressed people; the Plaza is swarming with happy pairs. There is a ‘coloured church’ adjoining our hotel. We are made well aware of the theological views of the coloured minister, as we lounge on the piazza; for, apparently under the impression that his flock are all deaf, he shouts his sermon with all the force of his stentorian lungs, and points each lesson with a roar.
