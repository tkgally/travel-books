# Chapter VIII.

I<sc>t</sc> was during my visit to the hut of the artist in sheepskin, which took place under the auspices of the shy but Polishly-civil painter, that the condition of the peasants, physical and moral, grew clearer to me. Their political condition was very difficult to find out anything about, but I knew that Galicia had a Diet, practically governed herself by means of a Parliament in which Jews, peasants, and Poles sat side by side, and that she sent up delegates and representatives to the Chamber in Vienna; the peasants owned their land, and shared communal rights to wood and grazing, &c. Persons of the landlord class were unknown in all the parts I visited: there were no gentlemen’s seats, nor a “big house” in any of the villages; no castles, ruined or otherwise, upon any of the mountains. Oppression, rack-renting, and evictions were unknown evils, of that I was assured; and beyond this, I felt that a political condition is not a bad one if you hear nothing of it.
