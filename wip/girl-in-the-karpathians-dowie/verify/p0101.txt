The peasants’ relation to the Jews is friendly, though perhaps to be deplored. Still, it seemed to me that to regret the domination of the Jews over the peasants in all business matters was to take hold of the wrong end of the stick. What one might regret is, that the Ruthenian is personally unpractical, unenterprising, and unambitious with regard to wealth, ease, and worldly advantage; though if he holds my view of these things, he will not regret these deficiencies, and will therefore be satisfied with the reigning systems. That the Jew handles money, imports goods, directs trade, and exploits labour almost exclusively, is not the hardship in a quiet Ruthenian village that it would be in one of our great centres, or even in one of our villages. For ideals differ. It is of little moment that the Ruthenian peasant should lose what he does not desire. Wealth—he cares little for it, and what it brings. He does not want to eat better food, or wear better clothes, and do less than the minimum of work that he already does. In all this he will be seen to differ materially from ourselves.
