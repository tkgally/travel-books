“Was there danger? . . . . . . and who ordered him?”

“There was no danger, but the Count, with whom he had arrived, wished us to have a guard of honour as a small mark of attention on his part, and a simliar relieving one would be found waiting at each station; his Excellency must be obeyed.”

So, whether we would or not, we were under the care of the celebrated <i>Compagni d’armi</i>, first-rate fellows who have all been brigands, enlisted by a master-stroke of the government in its service. Splendid pay, complete forgiveness, and a precedence to the military, were the bribes: they took, and brigandage was eradicated. Now the traveller is much more secure from violence than in England, the <i>Compagni</i> being responsible for all robberies, which are made good from their pay: but as they themselves were the thieves, there are no claimants; save when some foolish amateur tries an experiment, and is tracked and hooked up with an acuteness which shows how wise it is “to set a thief to catch a thief.” One would suspect the loyalty of such professionals; but a Sicilian respects his oath. Make one seriously pledge his word of honour, and you may trust him; it is not the <i>parole d’honneur</i> of the French.
