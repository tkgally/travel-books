Now on to the Palace! at whose doors a carriage is waiting for the present inhabitant, Prince Castelcicala, so long ambassador in England, become a very old man, and sinecure Lieutenant-Governor of Sicily, business affairs being really carried on by five directors, who represent the ministers of the Neapolitan Cabinet. The prettily painted royal apartments, with arched roofs, marble floors, and small quantity of classic furniture, give a good idea of a well-kept Italian residence: on a pillar is placed the last ram of Syracuse, which, with its bronze fellows, used to sit at the four corners of the town, and the wind rushing through their hollow bodies roared out of their mouths, telling from which quarter the Eolus came—an arrangement, they say, of Archimedes’.

The broad staircase with open colonnades and Moorish frescoes, leads to a chapel where a dim daylight all but conceals the thousand gems which form its arches, sides, and floor. Built in the eleventh century by Saracenic hands for Christian masters, entirely of minute mosaic set in gold, it is indescribably eccentric and beautiful.
