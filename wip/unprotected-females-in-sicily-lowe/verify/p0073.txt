Our night’s resting-place at Valla-lunga was much like the preceding one, save that the sounds of the <i>mandoline</i>, or Sicilian guitar, came from an apartment at the other end of the passage. Two velvet-clad gentlemen were sitting with the host and his family over a <i>bracciera</i>, singing the sorrows of a pretty nun. They perceived our interest, and in the most courteous manner, after seating us and falling into the back-ground, offered to sing a still more melancholy air; one of their countryman Bellini’s.

“You could not choose better,” we answered; and they commenced:—

“Did you see that fair young face change, fade, die? . . . yet Donna Lauretta was brilliant as the Persian rose but an hour ago. She was betrothed to Don Sylvestro, who, generous and noble, had made her guileless heart for ever his own. ‘I will away to the town,’ said he, ‘to prepare my ducal halls for her who is to enchant and order there.’ She followed with her princely parents, and rested on the balcony to gaze by moonlight on the town which held her lover. . . .
