In Ueno Park, another beautiful spot in Tokyo, rest six more Shoguns. And at Nikko, close to the celebrated founder of his race, lies one Shogun, the third of the line. That completes the list, and to each of them is erected a mausoleum in itself a complete work of art—national monuments of priceless value untouched by the hand of decay, buried amidst colossal trees, the brilliant memorials of Old Japan.

Thinking over this great past, a feeling almost of resentment arises within one against the ‘agent of a brand-new civilisation.’ Old Japan is disappearing fast, and the New Japan seems in danger of becoming the Ramsgate and Margate of the American population. To hear a Carson City counter-jumper expatiating on his exalted position as a ‘free-born ’Merukun citizen’ under the shadow of the tomb of the first Shogun brings ancient dignity and modern vulgarity into sufficiently strong contrast. Most travellers would perhaps rather be spared the contrast.
