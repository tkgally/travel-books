## VI.

As devoted to the earthly and eternal interests of a human being, the Tomb of Thi, next visited, naturally took stronger hold on sympathies rife in all hearts to-day than a tomb for deified bulls. Truly, the world offers few more impressive experiences than to be riding over an expanse of barren sand-hills, and then, suddenly, to come upon a vast excavation, to descend its incline to the portal once on a level with the whole city of the dead, and then to find one’s self ushered into the pictured interior of an Egyptian mortuary home. “The Egyptians,” says Diodorus, “call their houses hostelries, on account of the short period during which they inhabit them; but they call their tombs eternal dwelling-places.”

Profoundly one feels this as he wanders through the silent chambers, gazes on the infinitely varied scenes depicted on the walls, and tries to get into touch with a fellow-mortal, who, though he died five thousand years ago, seems so on hand to welcome one to his abiding home. Yes, the home feeling of the Egyptian tomb!
