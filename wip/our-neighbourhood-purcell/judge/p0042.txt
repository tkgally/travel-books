Can any man hold forth more learnedly on <i>hanazakari</i>, the flower in full bloom, the prince of wines,—the <i>yebisz dai</i>, with the red carp saltant stamped upon the cask—the <i>ôtari</i>, with the character <i>dai</i> beside it, and the representation of a target pierced through the centre with an arrow—the <i>musô ichi</i>, or unrivalled, and the <i>fukubôtan?</i> Does he not carry on his daily work beneath a sign whereon may be deciphered that “here may be procured the <i>san toku shu</i>,” or <i>saké</i> of three virtues, which keeps out the cold, appeases the hunger, and wraps in sleep him that drinketh thereof? He's a quaint-looking fellow truly, is our tapster! He has as many sides and angles as a prism. Regarded from the right he is a man of intelligence and ability, his eye is piercing and his look bright. From the left however, he's a blank and whimsical absurdity, all vacuity. His wandering, aimless orb is ever skimming helplessly about, lighting upon nothing, but fluttering like a swallow at sea. Address yourself to his right side, and you find yourself in conversation with a man of parts; cross over to the left and you feel inclined to adopt an infantile syllabary or humour him as a <i>crétin</i>. Observe him by the front aspect, and you are face to face with a riddle.
