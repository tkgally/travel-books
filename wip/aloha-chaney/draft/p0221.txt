Better the sympathy and comfort of an asylum, despite its sickly and pitiful companionship, than the monotonous fare, hard lodging, and rude “Greek” of this out-of-the-way settlement.

Happily, we had no sickness to humor in our choice of clime or dwelling-place. Caring chiefly to be let alone, we found in Mr. Barrett’s house on the beach the very best possible spot on earth for this sweet neglect. Our only fellow-boarder there was a delightfully shy man, who seldom appeared, except at meal-time, and whose unquestioning eating of whatever was placed before him seemed to rebuke the objections which sometimes rose within us. After doing us this good service, he would utterly disappear. The utmost that could be extracted from him in the way of friendly communication was his acceptance of the reading of a huge bundle of newspapers which had overtaken us at Hilo, and come to Kona with the other baggage. I have rarely met so agreeable a person in all my experience at public hotels. But “What’s his name or where’s his hame?” let no man ask me.
