[[cont]]Portuguese roads, their freedom from brigands, and the anxiety of the country-people to put us again on our right track, called forth many an expression of satisfaction on the part of my companions. I infer from it that such things are not, at all events at present, to be reckoned amongst the “<i>cosas d’España.</i>”

An attempt is being made to bring Busaco into notice as a rival to Cintra, as a place of fashionable resort; and its beauties are vaunted as not only greatly surpassing those of that far-famed spot, but as “exciting in the beholder mingled feelings of such rapturous admiration, and profound religious awe, that no language has words of sufficient force and meaning to give expression to them.” The <i>prestige</i> which Cintra has so long justly enjoyed of being a sort of earthly Eden has perhaps been due almost as much to its proximity to the capital and to its being the only one of the many similarly beautiful spots in the kingdom hitherto tolerably easy of access to the <i>Lisbonense</i>, as to its own real loveliness. For the natural beauties of the woods of Busaco have not yet sufficed to attract any considerable number of visitors, though the railroad has much diminished the fatigue of the journey. But Busaco lies many miles distant from the main line, and if you would reach it you must be content to undergo the penance of being squeezed for some hours in a lumbering <i>diligencia</i>, or take a private conveyance, and many persons consider this a great drawback from the pleasure of the excursion.
