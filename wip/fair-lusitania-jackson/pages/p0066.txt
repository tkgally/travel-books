Virginie of Lisbon, has her <i>atelier</i> in that favoured <i>quartier;</i> and double eye-glasses may be had there of a first-rate optician. Also, <i>bottines de Paris</i> and pretty <i>chinellas</i> for the <i>pés pequenos</i> of Portugal and Spain—the <i>senhoras</i> claiming to place themselves in this matter on a smaller footing than either the French or American ladies; while they generously credit our countrywomen with a larger proportion of understanding than has been awarded to them by either of those rivals. But to this claim of theirs I take occasion to put in a “demurrer,” having recently been favoured with a sight of the boots of a <i>bella condessa</i> and a pretty <i>senhorita</i>, and I solemnly protest that some Englishwomen of the same stature might have put both their feet into them. So much for <i>prestige</i>. <i>En revanche</i>, I allow that the said boots had heels twice the height of the highest of any I had seen before. It is a prevailing fashion here for the ladies thus to exalt themselves; but I would assure their <i>Excellencias</i> that it is one which detracts from, rather than adds to the grace and elegance of their carriage, as with tottering steps they pick their way up and down the steep Chiado. The height gained by the heel is lost in the stoop, which often gives a comical and undue prominence to the “<i>pouf</i>” of the dress, and much exaggeration to the “Grecian bend,” thus affording amusement, as I have sometimes observed, to profane lookers-on of the opposite sex.
