But it is all in vain; you may “hurry no man’s cattle” here. What, indeed, brings carriages so far down the Rua das Flores? They do not attempt to pass over the precipice dividing it from the Rua de S. João; for the drivers are not such famous whips as are the Jehus of Lisbon, who think nothing of rattling furiously down the perpendicular streets of that city. You have only to keep quiet and, as they tell you, “<i>não tem duvida minha Senhora,</i>” and in a trice, behold! there you are, safe at the bottom; the sensation of rushing down having been rather pleasurable than otherwise.

But let us look at the shops. Every morning we have passed an hour or two in <i>flânerie</i> between the Ruas das Flores and dos Clerigos. On the left are the goldsmiths, this street answering to the “Gold-street” of Lisbon; but here we have a far more profuse display of the gold filagree worn by the country-people. There are ear-rings of immense size, lockets and large crosses to match, with massive chains and rings, various in pattern but all of Moorish design. If the workmanship is not always of the most delicate kind—though beautiful specimens of the goldsmith’s art are to be obtained—the gold is of far superior quality to that used in England, even for expensive jewellery, and may not be sold unstamped.
