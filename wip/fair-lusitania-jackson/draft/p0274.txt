[[cont]]the Anglo-Portuguese—the Conde de J., as I have since learned—was much surprised and concerned to find that no one was waiting to meet me. However, as a <i>trem</i>, or carriage, was all I required, he was good enough to hail one that was passing.

“Where shall de coach take you <i>Excellencia</i> home to?” he asked. “To the Praça do Commercio, the station of the <i>caminho de ferro de l’este.</i>” “<i>Pois entaõ!</i>” he exclaimed, in astonishment; “the voyage of you <i>Excellencia</i> not finish den?” “No, I am going to Evora.” “<i>A Evora!</i> You <i>marido</i>, you husband, is dere den?” “Oh, no, I am going only to look at the old churches.” “In de vorld,” he exclaimed, “dere is no lady like de Eenglish for corage! Good night, and good <i>viagem à vossa Excellencia.</i>” And so we parted.

I was but barely in time for the steamer that carries passengers over to the Barreiro station for the evening train. But my courage had partly evaporated as the shades of evening came on, and therefore I took a ticket for Setubal only, promising myself to go on to Evora by the first morning <i>comboi.</i> I had the railway carriage to myself. On arriving at Setubal, or St. Ube’s, as the English have named it, I inquired of the most grave and important looking of the railway officials which was the best hotel.
