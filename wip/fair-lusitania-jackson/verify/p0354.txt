Passing along the Rua do Triumpho, and by yet two other extinct convents, San Bento and Carmo—now converted into very fine barracks, with a terrace and <i>alameda</i> planted with trees—we arrive at the entrance of the Crystal Palace, on the lofty esplanade of the Torre de Marco, the extreme west of the upper city. I had already visited that lovely spot; once I toiled up to it by the steep hill from the river side, and very hard work I found it for a hot summer’s morning, though the <i>Americano</i> had brought me from Foz to Masserellos. But I got through it pretty well; here and there a stray stone or projection of the hill affording a resting-place, and better still, every turn of the winding road, as I ascended higher and higher, disclosing a prospect of ever-increasing beauty, variety, and extent.

There is no permanent bazaar at the Palace, and as just now nothing particular is going on there, we had to pay but a <i>vintem</i> each for admission. It is an elegant building, but small compared with our Sydenham glass-house. The walls are of Oporto granite, the dome, and roofing of the side avenues iron and glass. It was designed by an English architect, Mr. Shields. It is square, each side measuring 110 <i>metres</i>, and it has a turreted pavilion at either end of the <i>façade</i>. There is a magnificent concert-room, containing a very fine organ.
