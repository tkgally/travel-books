I spent some little time in Buenos Aires with my brother and his family after my arrival, and again spent some ten days there by myself the following June, when I was leaving; so altogether I was able to see and to photograph a good deal.

My recollections of the city from my visit of 1888 were somewhat faint; but two important changes I did notice. The first was in the sanitary arrangements in the hotels; and of this I will say no more than that everything was now as it should be.

The second was in the paving of the streets, and in the trams.

In 1888 all the streets were paved so roughly that no carriage could drive along them save at walking-pace. There were some lines for horse-trams, and all the carriages were built of the right width for these; so that a carriage would trot along, the wheels on the rails, and would have to stop and pull out of the way when a tram came.

Further, the tram-horses were wretched, and one more than suspected that there was much brutality in the way in which they were used—and used up.
