Nothing in particular was going on when I arrived; but in October there was cutting of alfalfa and cebadilla for hay and silo. [This cebadilla <i>ought</i> to mean “barley-grass”; but it doesn’t! It means a native oat-grass whose seeds are black (they can be threshed out). Proper oats, such as we make porridge of, are called avena.] Of the cutting with machines there is nothing to record; nor can one say anything that is new of the haystacks, save that, in the present wasteful Argentine way (for there is land in abundance, but labour is relatively scarce) the stacks were never thatched.

But the silo was more interesting. To dig a pit for the green herbage as in England would have required too much labour; as would also the subsequent hoisting out of the silo when the cattle were to be fed. So the silo was made in a stack.

The rough rule appeared to be to put on about 1½ metres depth of the green stuff, and then to wait until the temperature half-way down this had (through fermentation) risen to 50° C. (122° Fahr.). Then another layer was put on.
