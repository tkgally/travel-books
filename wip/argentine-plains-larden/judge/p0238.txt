When the bicho is about to turn into a chrysalis it anchors itself very firmly to a small twig. And so tough is the silk that the twig in growing cannot stretch it. At the place of fastening such a twig shows a thickening, and on breaking it here one finds a flattened disc of silk with the original small hole in the middle. The twig has thickened on each side of the ligature, but has not been able to stretch it. This throttling of the twigs must injure the trees much. Outside, the cocoon is covered more or less with a very flimsy and not tough coating of silk. To do this I think the bicho must come out at night. Mr. Stuart Pennington says (I condense what he told me) that the male is an inconspicuous grayish-brown moth with feathery antennæ.[[fnref 1]] The female undergoes the final transformation in the “basket,” which she never leaves. She is a legless, almost organless insect with only a very few tufts of feathers scattered about her

[[fn 1]] I have read that all male moths that have to hunt up and discover non-flying females have feathery antennæ and that they discover them even when hidden. These feathery antennæ, <i>if we speak in terms of senses known to us</i>, would appear to be in some sense organs of scent.
